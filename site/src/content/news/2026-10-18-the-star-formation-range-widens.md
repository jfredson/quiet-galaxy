## What happened

On 17 October 2026 an outside model, Google's Gemini 3.1 Pro, was asked to argue with every one of the seven ranges under the site's review protocol, and filed twenty items; John ruled on all of them the same day, accepting every recommended ruling ([the rulings](https://github.com/jfredson/quiet-galaxy/blob/main/docs/reviews/2026-10-17-rulings.md)). One item changed a number. The [first weekly reading](/reading/), on 16 October, had found a 2023 measurement from the gamma-ray glow of radioactive aluminium that works out to 8 to 16 stars born in the galaxy each year, above the top of the range, which was 10; the range was held until the review could weigh it. The review recommended raising the top to 20, and John accepted.

## What it changes here

| what | before | after |
|---|---|---|
| [How many stars are born in the galaxy each year](/factors/star-formation/) | 1 to 10 a year | 1 to 20 a year, marked reviewed |
| The middle of the spread, detectable civilisations in the galaxy | 0.016 | 0.021 |
| The chance we are alone in the galaxy | 58.8 percent | 58.0 percent |
| Ranges reviewed | first pass on every range | every range reviewed |

## Why

The range's test says it takes a published measurement of the galaxy's star formation, and the 2023 measurement is one ([the range's page](/factors/star-formation/)). The top went to 20 rather than 16 because the measurement is one method, and its authors flag a possible foreground that could make the true figure lower, so the range takes a round step past the measurement rather than ending on it. Doubling the top of a well-measured piece moved the answer by less than one point, because the pieces that decide the spread are the ones nobody has measured ([the method](/method/)).

## What it does not mean

It does not mean the galaxy has more civilisations than it did; a range on this site is a statement about what the published work supports, and this one widened. It does not mean the review found the other six ranges right: it argued with each and John declined the other changes, several because they would have turned one author's bound into half the galaxy's answer ([the rulings](https://github.com/jfredson/quiet-galaxy/blob/main/docs/reviews/2026-10-17-rulings.md)). And a move from 58.8 to 58.0 percent is inside the day-to-day noise of a spread this wide ([the headline file](/data/headline.json)).
