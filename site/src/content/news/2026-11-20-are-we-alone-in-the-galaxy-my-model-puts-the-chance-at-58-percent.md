In [the post that opened this news section](/news/2026-10-18-start-here/), I set out why I built The Quiet Galaxy: to keep an honest count of what we actually know about whether anyone else is out there, and to change the count only when the evidence does. I built a model that takes the seven quantities of the [Drake equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drake_equation), from how many stars the galaxy makes each year to how long a civilisation stays detectable, and gives each one the range that published estimates support. The model makes a random pick from each range, multiplies the seven picks together, and does that two hundred thousand times ([how the numbers are worked out](/about/#method)). It currently puts the chance that we are the only civilisation in our galaxy that could be detected right now at 58 percent. For the whole observable universe, which the model takes to be 100 billion galaxies like ours, it puts the chance at 39 percent ([the numbers as they stand](/data/headline.json)). In this piece, "alone" always means that: the only civilisation that could be detected now.

The method follows [a 2018 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.02404) by [Anders Sandberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anders_Sandberg), [Eric Drexler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K._Eric_Drexler) and [Toby Ord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toby_Ord). Worked out the same way, the paper's own figures are 52 percent for the galaxy and 38 percent for the observable universe ([the 2018 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.02404)), close to the model's 58 and 39 ([the numbers as they stand](/data/headline.json)). The gap comes from my ranges for four of the seven quantities, which differ from the paper's: how many stars are born each year, the share of stars with planets, the number of planets per star that could support life, and the share of living planets where intelligence arises ([the comparison in the method notes](https://github.com/jfredson/quiet-galaxy/blob/main/docs/method.md)). This piece says what the 58 percent means, which quantity it leans on most, and a recent argument about that part of the story.

It helps to be clear about what the 58 percent is. It can look as if a number below 100 percent is a measure of doubt, so that the 42 percent left over is the room that remains because nobody has proved we are alone. That is not how this number is made. Each of the two hundred thousand runs is one galaxy that the published ranges allow. In 58 percent of those runs the picks multiply out to fewer than one civilisation, and in the other 42 percent they multiply out to one or more ([the numbers as they stand](/data/headline.json)). So the 42 percent is the share of allowed galaxies that hold company. Most of that spread comes from one quantity, whether life starts at all on a planet where it could, which nobody has measured. As the first post showed, changing nothing but how the model spreads its guesses across that range moves the chance that we are alone in the galaxy anywhere from 38 to 94 percent ([the range for whether life starts](/factors/life/)).

Because the answer leans hardest on the early steps, the start of life and the rise of intelligence, it matters whether those steps really are unlikely. One published reason to think so comes from Earth's own history. It is the hard-steps argument, which the physicist [Brandon Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brandon_Carter) first made in 1983. That argument says that because intelligence took so long to arise on Earth, several of the steps on the way to it must have been unlikely, and some of them seem to have happened only once. [A 2025 review in Science Advances](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.ads5698), by four researchers, says that this reading may be mistaken, for three reasons.

The first reason is that the planet may have set the pace. By the review's account, too little oxygen most likely ruled out complex cells and animals for the first 52 percent of Earth's history, and ruled out humans for the first 91 percent ([the 2025 review](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.ads5698)). On that account the steps came late because they could not have come sooner. The second reason is that "once" may be an accident of what survived, because early repeats of a step could have died out and left nothing behind. The review's example is an amoeba called [Paulinella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paulinella), which took in a light-harvesting bacterium of its own between 90 and 140 million years ago, although that step was long thought to have happened only a single time ([the 2025 review](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.ads5698)). The third reason is that the first living things to fill a role may crowd out later attempts at the same step, so that a step can be easy and still show up only once.

The review does not claim to have settled this. Its authors say they have not disproved the hard-steps argument, only put forward an alternative that can be tested, and they name the study of planets around other stars as the work that could tell the two apart ([the 2025 review](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.ads5698)). If the alternative holds up, the argument that intelligence must be rare because it took so long here loses its force. The review gives no figure for the start of life or for intelligence, so it does not say which version of my range for life it supports, and I have not changed any range because of it.

So the 58 percent answers one question: given what has been published about stars, planets, life and minds, how likely is it that nobody else could be detected ([the numbers as they stand](/data/headline.json))? It changes only when a published finding changes one of the seven ranges ([how the numbers are worked out](/about/#method)), and the range it leans on hardest, whether life starts, is the one nobody has measured ([the range for whether life starts](/factors/life/)). The 2025 review is the clearest published case that the early steps may be less hard than Earth's history seems to say. It gives no figure, so it moves no range yet, but it names the kind of work that could.
