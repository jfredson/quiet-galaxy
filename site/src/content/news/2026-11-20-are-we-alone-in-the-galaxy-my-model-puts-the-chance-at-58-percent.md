In [the post that opened this news section](/news/2026-10-18-start-here/), I set out how The Quiet Galaxy keeps count. I built a model that takes the seven quantities of the [Drake equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drake_equation), from how many stars the galaxy makes each year to how long a civilisation stays detectable, and gives each one the range that published estimates support. The model makes a random pick from each range, multiplies the seven picks together, and does that two hundred thousand times ([how the numbers are worked out](/about/#method)). It currently puts the chance that we are the only civilisation in our galaxy that could be detected right now at 58 percent. For the whole observable universe, which the model takes to be 100 billion galaxies like ours, it puts the chance at 39 percent ([the numbers as they stand](/data/headline.json)). In this piece, "alone" always means that: the only civilisation that could be detected now.

That first post followed [a 2018 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.02404) by [Anders Sandberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anders_Sandberg), [Eric Drexler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K._Eric_Drexler) and [Toby Ord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toby_Ord), whose method the model uses, and it ended on the paper's main point: done this way, the silence looks like roughly what you should expect, given how little we know ([the post that opened this news section](/news/2026-10-18-start-here/)). This piece takes up something the model leaves out on purpose: the fact that we have looked and found nobody. The model's numbers come only from what has been published about stars, planets, life and minds. The question here is why the silence is not counted too, and what happens when it is.

First, it helps to be clear about what the 58 percent is. It can look as if a number below 100 percent is a measure of doubt, so that the 42 percent left over is the room that remains because nobody has proved we are alone. That is not how this number is made. Each of the two hundred thousand runs is one galaxy that the published ranges allow. In 58 percent of those runs the picks multiply out to fewer than one civilisation, and in the other 42 percent they multiply out to one or more ([the numbers as they stand](/data/headline.json)). So the 42 percent is the share of allowed galaxies that hold company. Most of that spread comes from one quantity, whether life starts at all on a planet where it could, which nobody has measured. As the first post showed, changing nothing but how the model spreads its guesses across that range moves the chance that we are alone in the galaxy anywhere from 38 to 94 percent ([the range for whether life starts](/factors/life/)).

The silence itself is easy to state. No search has picked up a signal from another civilisation, and nobody has settled our part of the galaxy or left any sign of having been here. A search that finds nothing can still count as evidence. A quarter of the model's runs are crowded galaxies, with more than 420 civilisations in each ([the numbers as they stand](/data/headline.json)). If the civilisations in them were loud, or spread from star to star, we would probably have noticed one by now. Because we have not, those crowded galaxies become harder to believe, and what is left leans towards few civilisations or none. The trouble is the word "if". How much the silence rules out depends on how other civilisations would behave: whether they would send signals we could pick up, whether they would spread, and whether we would recognise the signs if they had. Those are guesses about behaviour, not quantities that published measurements pin down, and the model takes in only ranges that published estimates support ([how the numbers are worked out](/about/#method)). So the model leaves the silence out on purpose, and the 58 percent is a figure from before it is counted.

The 2018 paper did count the silence, and its results show how much rests on those guesses. Before the silence is counted, the paper puts the chance that we are alone at 52 percent for the galaxy and 38 percent for the observable universe. Counting the radio searches, which have covered very little so far ([we have not looked long enough](/explanations/not-looked-long-enough/)), moves those figures by only a few points. Counting the fact that nobody has settled our part of the galaxy moves them to 99.6 percent for the galaxy and 85 percent for the universe ([the 2018 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.02404)). That version tells us a great deal only if civilisations would spread across the galaxy, and only if we would see the signs where one had. So most of the rise rests on that one guess. My page on the paper gives its reading of why the figures rise: once the silence is counted, more of the uncertainty sits on the early steps, the start of life and the rise of intelligence ([why the paper says there is no paradox](/explanations/no-paradox-just-wide-ranges/)).

The model's starting figures sit close to the paper's starting figures: 58 percent against 52 for the galaxy, and 39 percent against 38 for the universe ([the numbers as they stand](/data/headline.json)). The gap comes from my ranges for four of the seven quantities, which differ from the paper's: how many stars are born each year, the share of stars with planets, the number of planets per star that could support life, and the share of living planets where intelligence arises ([the comparison in the method notes](https://github.com/jfredson/quiet-galaxy/blob/main/docs/method.md)). The model does not run the paper's second step, and that is a choice: the step needs a guess about how civilisations behave, and the model's ranges come only from published estimates. The 58 and the 39 are figures from before the silence is counted, and they are meant to be.

Whichever way the silence is treated, the answer leans hardest on the early steps, the start of life and the rise of intelligence, so it matters whether those steps really are unlikely. One published reason to think so comes from Earth's own history. It is the hard-steps argument, which the physicist [Brandon Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brandon_Carter) first made in 1983. That argument says that because intelligence took so long to arise on Earth, several of the steps on the way to it must have been unlikely, and some of them seem to have happened only once. [A 2025 review in Science Advances](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.ads5698), by four researchers, says that this reading may be mistaken, for three reasons.

The first reason is that the planet may have set the pace. By the review's account, too little oxygen most likely ruled out complex cells and animals for the first 52 percent of Earth's history, and ruled out humans for the first 91 percent ([the 2025 review](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.ads5698)). On that account the steps came late because they could not have come sooner. The second reason is that "once" may be an accident of what survived, because early repeats of a step could have died out and left nothing behind. The review's example is an amoeba called [Paulinella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paulinella), which took in a light-harvesting bacterium of its own between 90 and 140 million years ago, although that step was long thought to have happened only a single time ([the 2025 review](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.ads5698)). The third reason is that the first living things to fill a role may crowd out later attempts at the same step, so that a step can be easy and still show up only once.

The review does not claim to have settled this. Its authors say they have not disproved the hard-steps argument, only put forward an alternative that can be tested, and they name the study of planets around other stars as the work that could tell the two apart ([the 2025 review](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.ads5698)). If the alternative holds up, the argument that intelligence must be rare because it took so long here loses its force. The review gives no figure for the start of life or for intelligence, so it does not say which version of my range for life it supports, and I have not changed any range because of it.

So the 58 percent answers one question: given what has been published about stars, planets, life and minds, how likely is it that nobody else could be detected ([the numbers as they stand](/data/headline.json))? It changes only when a published finding changes one of the seven ranges ([how the numbers are worked out](/about/#method)). It leaves the silence out on purpose, because how much an empty sky tells us depends on how other civilisations would behave, and no published measurement settles that. [The first post](/news/2026-10-18-start-here/) said that, counted this way, the silence is roughly what you should expect. The 2018 paper shows what counting the silence gives once a guess about behaviour is made, and that most of its rise comes from the guess that civilisations would spread. The 2025 review is the clearest published case that the early steps, on which the answer leans hardest, may be less hard than Earth's history seems to say.
