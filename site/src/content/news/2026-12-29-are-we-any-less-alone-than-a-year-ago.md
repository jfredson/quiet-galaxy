On July 10th, 2026, astronomers reported what the [James Webb Space Telescope](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/) saw when it looked closely at two of the best candidates anyone had for an alien megastructure. In 2024 [a search of five million stars](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.02927) had picked out seven small red stars that give off more infrared heat than a star of their kind should, which is the glow a [Dyson sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyson_sphere), a swarm of collectors built around a star to catch its light, would shed. Webb found that for two of the seven the extra heat comes from galaxies far in the background, so nearly in line with the stars that the older survey telescope could not tell them apart ([Zackrisson and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.09460)). A second paper the same month found the other five still unexplained, and none of them shown to be artificial ([Korn and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.25701)).

That was the shape of the whole year in the search for life and minds beyond Earth: the hints that looked strongest faded when someone looked harder, and the rest stayed open. The Quiet Galaxy asks the physicist [Enrico Fermi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrico_Fermi)'s question of why we see no one else, by multiplying together the seven ranges of the [Drake equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drake_equation) two hundred thousand times and counting how often the answer comes out below one civilisation. Nothing published in 2026 moved any of those ranges. The chance that we are alone in the galaxy stands at 58.0 percent, and the answer in the middle of the spread is 0.021 civilisations we could detect, about one for every 47 galaxies like ours ([the numbers as they stand](/data/headline.json)).

## What the year found

**Rocky planets around small stars keep turning up bare.** Most stars in the galaxy are dim red dwarfs ([the share of stars with planets](/factors/planets/)), so whether their rocky planets can hold on to air matters for how many places life could start. In April, Webb's measurements of the heat from TRAPPIST-1 b and c, two planets of [the TRAPPIST-1 system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TRAPPIST-1), strongly disfavoured any atmosphere as thick as Earth's on either ([Gillon and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41550-026-02806-9)). In November, Webb's [Rocky Worlds programme](https://rockyworlds.stsci.edu/) found no substantial atmosphere on LTT 1445 A b, a rocky planet of [the nearest such system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LTT_1445), in a paper not yet through peer review ([Ih and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17300)). All three orbit closer to their stars than the band where water can stay liquid. The one planet inside that band with news this year, [LHS 1140 b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LHS_1140_b), showed helium escaping from it in 2024 and not in 2025, which the authors read as an upper atmosphere made mostly of helium, with heavier gases held lower down; the finding says nothing about water ([Cherubim and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aea9708)). A measurement of how often such planets keep their air is exactly what would move [the range for planets per star that could support life](/factors/habitable-planets/), which runs from 1 in 100 to 1. So far the results are single planets, mostly too hot to count, so the range held.

**Mars gave more to explain and no answer.** The most discussed possible sign of life of recent years is a rock called Cheyava Falls, in which the [Perseverance rover](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/mars-2020-perseverance/) found features that its team, [in a paper published in September 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09413-0), called potential signs of life. In June 2026 the same rocks were shown to hold hundreds of detections of carbon-based matter, and that paper says its measurements cannot tell whether the carbon came from life or from chemistry without it ([Murphy and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adx0047)). Also in June, a laboratory grew pale spots like the ones in Cheyava Falls by adding microbes to iron-rich sand, and none appeared in sterile samples; the authors say further experiments are needed to learn whether chemistry without life can make them too ([Zielinska and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1177/15311074261464015)). In February, a team worked out that long chains of carbon found by the [Curiosity rover](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/msl-curiosity/) in Gale crater were once thousands of times more abundant than they are now, more than falling dust and meteorites can readily explain, and left open both a source without life and one with it ([Pavlov and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1177/15311074261417879)). Settling any of this means studying the rock on Earth, and in January the US Congress passed a budget that, as Live Science reported, [effectively cancelled the mission to bring the samples home](https://www.livescience.com/space/mars/nasas-mars-sample-return-is-dead-leaving-china-to-retrieve-signs-of-life-from-the-red-planet). A sign of life beyond Earth would move [the range for whether life starts](/factors/life/), the piece that matters most of all seven. Signs that their own authors call open do not.

**The listening stayed silent.** China's [FAST radio telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five-hundred-meter_Aperture_Spherical_Telescope) listened for about four hours to [3I/ATLAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3I/ATLAS), the third object seen passing through the solar system from another star, and heard nothing, though it would have caught a transmitter on board as weak as a few thousandths of a watt ([Li and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ae8025)). The [SETI Institute](https://www.seti.org/)'s [Allen Telescope Array](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allen_Telescope_Array) began listening at the point in the sky opposite the Sun, where someone watching Earth cross the Sun would be, and is about 12 percent of the way round with no detection ([Gerrard and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07065)). A search of 129 nearby galaxies found none glowing with the waste heat that swarms of starlight collectors would shed ([Curtis and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12458)). Silences like these limit all seven pieces multiplied together, not any one of them, so on their own they move no range ([the share of civilisations we could detect](/factors/detectable/)).

**The first step of life looked a little easier in the laboratory.** In February, a group reported an RNA molecule only 45 building blocks long that can copy its own partner strand and, from pieces, itself, though the two halves have not yet been joined into a full self-copying cycle ([Gianni and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adt2760)). How improbable that first step was is one of the things the range for whether life starts is waiting on, and one step short of the cycle is not yet the answer.

## What the site found since it went live

The site began on October 16th, 2026, and the biggest change since had nothing to do with the sky. Its first calculation, before it went public, said 88 percent alone; the same day it switched to the published way of spreading the uncertainty about whether life starts, and the answer fell to 59 percent ([the opening post](/news/2026-10-18-start-here/)). The next day a 2023 study of radioactive aluminium in the galaxy raised the top of the range for how many stars are born each year from 10 to 20, and the chance of being alone moved from 58.8 to 58.0 percent ([how that happened](/news/2026-10-18-the-star-formation-range-widens/)). Since then [the weekly reading](/reading/) has run every Saturday and moved nothing, and in November a pass through ten journals back to January read 706 titles and logged nine papers that the weekly searches had missed, five of them in this post ([the list of changes](https://github.com/jfredson/quiet-galaxy/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md)). The lesson of the year is the one the numbers kept giving: the answer leans hardest on whether life starts at all, a range fifty powers of ten wide, and nothing in 2026 measured it.

## Coming up in 2027

**Gaia's fourth data release, December 2nd, 2026.** The European Space Agency's [Gaia mission](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Gaia) releases its fourth catalogue on that date ([the release schedule](https://www.cosmos.esa.int/web/gaia/release)), including orbits for companions of stars down to giant planets ([what it contains](https://www.cosmos.esa.int/web/gaia/dr4)). Working through it is the first job of the new year for [the share of stars with planets](/factors/planets/), though giant planets alone will not settle how many stars have none.

**Roman's first planet-hunting season, planned for February 13th to April 25th, 2027.** NASA's [Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/roman-space-telescope/) [launched on August 30th, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/roman-space-telescope/roman-launch/), and the first season of its survey of the crowded middle of the galaxy is planned for that window, subject to change ([the observing plan](https://roman-docs.stsci.edu/roman-community-defined-surveys/roman-observations-in-the-first-two-years-of-science-operations)). Over the whole survey it aims to find more than a thousand planets on wide orbits by the way their gravity briefly brightens a star behind them ([NASA's survey page](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/roman-space-telescope/core-community-surveys/)). A count of that kind, across ordinary stars, is the test for [the share of stars with planets](/factors/planets/), but the season starts the count and its results will take longer.

**The launch of Plato, early 2027.** The European Space Agency's [Plato](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Plato) will use 26 cameras to look for rocky planets as far out as the liquid-water band of stars like the Sun. ESA said in April that it is planned to launch in January 2027 ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Plato/Plato_aces_space-like_tests)), while the mission's own page says April 2027 ([ESA's Plato page](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Plato)), and in 2025 the German space agency expected it in late 2026 ([DLR](https://www.dlr.de/en/latest/news/2025/plato-mission-to-launch-in-late-2026-onboard-ariane-6)), so the date has already slipped once. A launch moves nothing here; years from now, its count of rocky planets around Sun-like stars could move [the range for planets per star that could support life](/factors/habitable-planets/).
