On July 10th, 2026, astronomers reported what the [James Webb Space Telescope](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/) saw when it looked closely at two stars that had been flagged as possible homes of an alien megastructure. In 2024 [a search of five million stars](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.02927) had picked out seven small red stars that give off more infrared heat than a star of their kind should, which is the glow a [Dyson sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyson_sphere), a swarm of collectors built around a star to catch its light, would shed. Webb found that for two of the seven the extra heat comes from galaxies far in the background, so nearly in line with the stars that the survey telescope used in 2024 could not tell the galaxies from the stars ([Zackrisson and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.09460)). A second paper the same month found the other five still unexplained, and none of them shown to be artificial ([Korn and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.25701)).

In the search for life and minds beyond Earth, 2026 went much the same way: where someone looked harder at a hint, it faded or stayed open, and none was confirmed. The Quiet Galaxy asks the physicist [Enrico Fermi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enrico_Fermi)'s question of why we see no one else, by taking the seven quantities in the [Drake equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drake_equation), giving each a range of values that published work supports, multiplying random picks from the seven ranges together two hundred thousand times, and counting how often the answer comes out below one civilisation. Nothing published in 2026 moved any of those ranges. The chance that we are alone in the galaxy stands at 58.0 percent, and half of the two hundred thousand answers fall below 0.021 civilisations we could detect, about one for every 47 galaxies like ours ([the numbers as they stand](/data/headline.json)).

## What the year found

**Rocky planets around small stars keep turning up bare.** Most stars in the galaxy are dim red dwarfs ([the share of stars with planets](/factors/planets/)), so whether their rocky planets can hold on to air matters for how many places life could start. In April, Webb's measurements of the heat from TRAPPIST-1 b and c, two planets of [the TRAPPIST-1 system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TRAPPIST-1), strongly disfavoured any atmosphere as thick as Earth's on either ([Gillon and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41550-026-02806-9)). In November, Webb's [Rocky Worlds programme](https://rockyworlds.stsci.edu/) found no substantial atmosphere on LTT 1445 A b, a rocky planet of [the nearest such system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LTT_1445), in a paper not yet through peer review ([Ih and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17300)). All three orbit closer to their stars than the band where water can stay liquid. The one planet inside that band with news this year, [LHS 1140 b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LHS_1140_b), showed helium escaping from it in 2024 and not in 2025, which the authors read as an upper atmosphere made mostly of helium, with heavier gases held lower down; the finding says nothing about water ([Cherubim and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aea9708)). A measurement of how often such planets keep their air is exactly what would move [the range for planets per star that could support life](/factors/habitable-planets/), which runs from 1 such planet for every 100 stars to 1 for every star. So far the results cover single planets, most of them too hot for liquid water, so the range did not change.

**Mars gave more to explain and no answer.** On Mars, the possible sign of life to watch is a rock called Cheyava Falls, in which the [Perseverance rover](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/mars-2020-perseverance/) found features that its team, [in a paper published in September 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09413-0), called potential signs of life. In June 2026 a second paper reported hundreds of detections of carbon-based matter in two rocks at the same site, and its authors say their measurements cannot tell whether the carbon came from life or from chemistry without life ([Murphy and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adx0047)). Also in June, a laboratory grew pale spots like the ones in Cheyava Falls by adding microbes to iron-rich sand, and none appeared in sterile samples; the authors say further experiments are needed to learn whether chemistry without life can make them too ([Zielinska and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1177/15311074261464015)). In February, a team worked out that long chains of carbon found by the [Curiosity rover](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/msl-curiosity/) in Gale crater were once thousands of times more abundant than they are now, more than falling dust and meteorites can readily explain, and left open both a source without life and one with it ([Pavlov and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1177/15311074261417879)). Settling whether life made any of these features means bringing the rover's rock samples to Earth to study, and in January the US Congress passed a budget that, as Live Science reported, [effectively cancelled the mission to bring the samples home](https://www.livescience.com/space/mars/nasas-mars-sample-return-is-dead-leaving-china-to-retrieve-signs-of-life-from-the-red-planet). A sign of life beyond Earth would move [the range for whether life starts](/factors/life/), the one of the seven that moves the answer most: changing only how the site spreads its guesses across that range moves the chance of being alone anywhere from 38 to 94 percent (the table on [that range's page](/factors/life/)). Signs whose own authors say the question is still open do not.

**The listening stayed silent.** China's [FAST radio telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five-hundred-meter_Aperture_Spherical_Telescope) listened for about four hours to [3I/ATLAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3I/ATLAS), the third object seen passing through the solar system from another star, and heard nothing, though it would have caught a transmitter on board as weak as a few thousandths of a watt ([Li and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ae8025)). The [SETI Institute](https://www.seti.org/)'s [Allen Telescope Array](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allen_Telescope_Array) began listening at the point in the sky opposite the Sun, where someone watching Earth cross the Sun would be, and has covered about 12 percent of the sky positions a full circuit needs, with no detection ([Gerrard and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07065)). A search of 129 nearby galaxies found none glowing with the waste heat that swarms of starlight collectors would shed ([Curtis and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12458)). A search that hears nothing limits how many civilisations there can be in all, which is the seven quantities multiplied together, but cannot say which of the seven is small, so on its own it moves none of the ranges ([the share of civilisations we could detect](/factors/detectable/)).

**A laboratory made a small RNA molecule that copies itself in two halves.** In February, a group reported an RNA molecule only 45 building blocks long that can copy its own partner strand and, from pieces, itself, though the two halves have not yet been joined into a full self-copying cycle ([Gianni and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adt2760)). How likely it is that a self-copying molecule arises by chance is one of the things the range for whether life starts is waiting on, and a molecule that has not yet copied itself in one full cycle does not answer it.

## What the site found since it went live

The site began on October 16th, 2026, and the change that has moved its answer most, by 29 points, had nothing to do with the sky. The site's first calculation, before it went public, said 88 percent alone; the same day the site switched to the way a 2018 paper spreads the uncertainty about whether life starts, and the answer fell to 59 percent ([the opening post](/news/2026-10-18-start-here/)). The next day, after an outside review weighed a 2023 study of radioactive aluminium in the galaxy, the top of the range for how many stars are born each year went from 10 to 20, and the chance of being alone moved from 58.8 to 58.0 percent ([how that happened](/news/2026-10-18-the-star-formation-range-widens/)). Since then [the site's weekly reading](/reading/), an automatic search every Saturday for new published work that bears on each range, has moved nothing, and in November a pass through ten journals back to January read 706 titles and logged nine papers that the weekly searches had missed, five of them in this post ([the list of changes](https://github.com/jfredson/quiet-galaxy/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md)). The lesson of the year is that the answer depends most on whether life starts at all, which swings the chance of being alone from 38 to 94 percent while doubling the top of the star range moved it by less than one point, and nothing in 2026 measured whether life starts.

## Coming up in 2027

**Gaia's fourth data release, December 2nd, 2026.** The European Space Agency's [Gaia mission](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Gaia) releases its fourth catalogue on that date ([the release schedule](https://www.cosmos.esa.int/web/gaia/release)), including orbits for companions of stars down to giant planets ([what it contains](https://www.cosmos.esa.int/web/gaia/dr4)). In the new year the site's weekly reading will go through the release for anything that bears on [the share of stars with planets](/factors/planets/), though giant planets alone will not settle how many stars have none.

**Roman's first planet-hunting season, planned for February 13th to April 25th, 2027.** NASA's [Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/roman-space-telescope/) [launched on August 30th, 2026](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/roman-space-telescope/roman-launch/), and the first season of its survey of the crowded middle of the galaxy is planned for that window, subject to change ([the observing plan](https://roman-docs.stsci.edu/roman-community-defined-surveys/roman-observations-in-the-first-two-years-of-science-operations)). Over the whole survey it aims to find more than a thousand planets on wide orbits by the way their gravity briefly brightens a star behind them ([NASA's survey page](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/roman-space-telescope/core-community-surveys/)). A count of that kind, across ordinary stars, is the evidence that would change the range for [the share of stars with planets](/factors/planets/), but the first season only starts the count, and its results will take longer.

**The launch of Plato, early 2027.** The European Space Agency's [Plato](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Plato) will use 26 cameras to look for rocky planets as far out as the liquid-water band of stars like the Sun. ESA said in April that it is planned to launch in January 2027 ([ESA](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Plato/Plato_aces_space-like_tests)), while the mission's own page says April 2027 ([ESA's Plato page](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Plato)), and in 2025 the German space agency expected it in late 2026 ([DLR](https://www.dlr.de/en/latest/news/2025/plato-mission-to-launch-in-late-2026-onboard-ariane-6)), so the date has already slipped once. A launch alone changes nothing on this site; years from now, Plato's count of rocky planets around Sun-like stars could move [the range for planets per star that could support life](/factors/habitable-planets/).
