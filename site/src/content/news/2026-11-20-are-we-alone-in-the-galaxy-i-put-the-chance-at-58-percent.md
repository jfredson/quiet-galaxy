The home page of The Quiet Galaxy carries two numbers. One is a 58 percent chance that we are the only civilisation in our galaxy that could be detected right now. The other is a 39 percent chance that we are the only one in the whole observable universe, which I take to be 100 billion galaxies like ours ([the numbers as they stand](/data/headline.json)). In this piece, "alone" always means that: the only civilisation that could be detected now. The piece says what the two numbers measure, and what they leave out.

Both numbers come from the [Drake equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drake_equation), the multiplication of seven quantities that the astronomer [Frank Drake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Drake) wrote down in 1961 to estimate how many civilisations we could detect. The quantities run from how many stars the galaxy makes each year to how long a civilisation stays detectable. For each one, I keep the range that published estimates support. The calculation makes a random pick from each of the seven ranges, multiplies the picks together, and does that two hundred thousand times ([how the numbers are worked out](/about/#method)). The 58 percent is the share of those runs that come out below one civilisation in our galaxy ([the numbers as they stand](/data/headline.json)). The method follows [a 2018 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.02404) by [Anders Sandberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anders_Sandberg), [Eric Drexler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K._Eric_Drexler) and [Toby Ord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toby_Ord).

The answers from those runs are spread very widely. The answer in the middle is 0.021 civilisations in our galaxy, which is about one for every 47 galaxies like ours, but a quarter of the runs come out above 420 civilisations and a quarter come out below one in ten to the thirty ([the numbers as they stand](/data/headline.json)). The quantity that moves the answer most is whether life starts at all on a planet where it could. Nobody has measured it, and the range I give it is fifty powers of ten wide ([the range for whether life starts](/factors/life/)). I tested how much rests on that range by running the calculation again with other versions of it. If every power of ten from one in a thousand up to certainty were equally likely, the chance that we are alone in the galaxy would be 38 percent. If the range ran down to one in ten to the fifty on equal terms, it would be 94 percent ([the range for whether life starts](/factors/life/)). By comparison, when I doubled the top of the range for how many stars are born each year, which is the best-measured of the seven quantities, on October 17th, 2026, the chance of being alone moved by less than one point, from 58.8 to 58.0 percent ([the star-formation post](/news/2026-10-18-the-star-formation-range-widens/)).

All of this uses what has been published about stars, planets, life and minds. None of it uses the fact that we have looked and found nobody. No search has picked up a signal from another civilisation, and nobody has settled our part of the galaxy or left any sign of having been here. The 58 percent and the 39 percent are what the published ranges give before that fact is counted ([the numbers as they stand](/data/headline.json)).

The 2018 paper went further and counted it, for its own figures. Before the silence is counted, the paper puts the chance that we are alone at 52 percent for the galaxy and 38 percent for the observable universe. After the silence is counted, the paper's figures rise to between 53 and 99.6 percent for the galaxy, and to between 39 and 85 percent for the universe, depending on what kind of evidence is used ([the 2018 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.02404)). Counting the radio searches moves the figures by only a few points. Counting the fact that nobody has settled our part of the galaxy moves them to 99.6 percent for the galaxy and 85 percent for the universe ([the 2018 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.02404)). In every version the paper reports, both figures go up, so the silence makes it more likely that we are alone in the galaxy and also more likely that we are alone in the universe. My page on the paper gives its reading of why: once the silence is counted, more of the uncertainty sits on the early steps, the start of life and the rise of intelligence ([why the paper says there is no paradox](/explanations/no-paradox-just-wide-ranges/)).

My own starting figures sit close to the paper's starting figures: 58 percent against 52 for the galaxy, and 39 percent against 38 for the universe ([the numbers as they stand](/data/headline.json)). The gap comes from my ranges for four of the seven quantities, which differ from the paper's: how many stars are born each year, the share of stars with planets, the number of planets per star that could support life, and the share of living planets where intelligence arises ([the comparison in the method notes](https://github.com/jfredson/quiet-galaxy/blob/main/docs/method.md)). I have not run the paper's second step on my own ranges, so the site has no figure of its own for after the silence is counted. The 58 and the 39 should be read as figures from before it.

There is also a published argument that any count of the silence has to reckon with. It is aimed at the hard-steps argument, which the physicist [Brandon Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brandon_Carter) first made in 1983. That argument says that because intelligence took so long to arise on Earth, several of the steps on the way to it must have been unlikely, and some of them seem to have happened only once. [A 2025 review in Science Advances](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.ads5698), by four researchers, says that this reading may be mistaken, for three reasons.

The first reason is that the planet may have set the pace. By the review's account, too little oxygen most likely ruled out complex cells and animals for the first 52 percent of Earth's history, and ruled out humans for the first 91 percent ([the 2025 review](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.ads5698)). On that account the steps came late because they could not have come sooner. The second reason is that "once" may be an accident of what survived, because early repeats of a step could have died out and left nothing behind. The review's example is an amoeba called [Paulinella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paulinella), which took in a light-harvesting bacterium of its own between 90 and 140 million years ago, although that step was long thought to have happened only a single time ([the 2025 review](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.ads5698)). The third reason is that the first living things to fill a role may crowd out later attempts at the same step, so that a step can be easy and still show up only once.

The review does not claim to have settled this. Its authors say they have not disproved the hard-steps argument, only put forward an alternative that can be tested, and they name the study of planets around other stars as the work that could tell the two apart ([the 2025 review](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.ads5698)). If the alternative holds up, the argument that intelligence must be rare because it took so long here loses its force ([the 2025 review](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.ads5698)). The review gives no figure for the start of life or for intelligence, so it does not say which version of my range for life it supports, and I have not changed any range because of it.

So the number on the home page answers one question: given what has been published, how likely is it that nobody else could be detected? It changes only when a published finding changes one of the seven ranges ([how the numbers are worked out](/about/#method)). It does not answer the second question, of what the empty sky adds. The 2018 paper is the clearest published answer to that second question for its own figures. In every version it reports, counting the silence raised both of its figures, and counting the fact that nobody has settled our part of the galaxy raised them to 99.6 percent for the galaxy and 85 percent for the universe ([the 2018 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.02404)).
