The Quiet Galaxy asks the question the physicist Enrico Fermi asked over lunch in 1950: [where is everybody?](/) The galaxy is old enough and big enough that someone should have shown up by now, and nobody has. In 1961 the astronomer Frank Drake wrote the question as a multiplication of seven quantities, so that a room of scientists could argue about one at a time, and the site takes it apart the same way.

A number is worth having here because of how far apart the guesses are. Take every hopeful estimate that has been published and you get 5 trillion civilisations in our galaxy; take every gloomy one and you get about 1 in 10 to the 67. So the site gives each piece the range the published estimates support, multiplies the seven two hundred thousand times with a random pick from each, and reports the spread. Done this way, following [a 2018 paper by Anders Sandberg, Eric Drexler and Toby Ord](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.02404), the silence looks like roughly what you should expect, given how little we know.

This is the start of the site's news section. When a weekly reading finds a result that moves a range, or a famous claim that turns out not to, I will write it up here beside the record it explains. A quiet week gets no post. An essay about the project is coming.

## Where the numbers stand

As of the run of 17 October 2026, there is a 42 percent chance that other detectable civilisations are in our galaxy right now, and a 61 percent chance that there are others somewhere in the observable universe. The middle of the spread is 0.021 detectable civilisations in our galaxy, which works out to one for every 47 galaxies like ours ([the headline file](/data/headline.json)).

The seven ranges behind those numbers, all now reviewed:

- how many stars are born in the galaxy each year: 1 to 20 ([the equation](/equation/));
- the share of stars that have planets: a half to all of them;
- planets per star that could support life: 0.01 to 1;
- the share of those planets on which life actually starts: centred on one start per planet, but fifty powers of ten wide;
- the share of planets with life on which it becomes intelligent: 1 in 10,000 to all of them;
- the share of intelligent species that make themselves detectable: 1 in 100 to all of them;
- how long a civilisation stays detectable: 100 years to 10 billion years.

One of the seven matters more than the others put together. Changing only the shape of the range for whether life starts moves the chance that we are alone in the galaxy anywhere from 38 to 94 percent ([the method](/method/)).

## How to follow it

Every Saturday evening, Pacific time, a fresh session searches for new work against each piece's test and each explanation's, logs the papers it finds, and gives every record a verdict. It may widen or narrow a range by at most one step of ten at either end in a single reading, and it may never touch the range for whether life starts; anything bigger comes to me.

Each week's reading is on [the Reading page](https://thequietgalaxy.com/reading/). [The Story](https://thequietgalaxy.com/story/) logs every publication read and whether it moved a range. [The Equation](https://thequietgalaxy.com/equation/) has a slider for each piece, so you can try your own numbers, and every explanation for the silence, thirty-two so far, has a page of its own under [Explanations](https://thequietgalaxy.com/explanations/). [The Method](https://thequietgalaxy.com/method/) explains how the numbers are worked out and who did what. If you think a range is wrong or an explanation is missing, [the Disagree page](https://thequietgalaxy.com/disagree/) has a form for each, and I rule on every one. [The feed](/news/feed.xml) carries every post in this section.

## The most interesting news so far

The biggest swing came before the site went public, from a single modelling choice. On 16 October 2026 [the first pass](/story/#logged-2026-10-16) came out at 88 percent alone in the galaxy. Using the 2018 paper's shape for the chance that life starts moved that to 59 percent the same day. Nothing in the sky had changed; what changed was how the site described what we do not know.

The first weekly reading, the same day, found a 2023 measurement by Siegert and colleagues, taken from the gamma-ray glow of radioactive aluminium, which works out to 8 to 16 stars born each year. That was above the top of the range, then 10. I kept the top at 10 until an outside review could weigh it. The review ran on 17 October 2026, and [the top went up to 20](/news/2026-10-18-the-star-formation-range-widens/): the measurement is one method, and its authors flag a possible foreground that could make the true figure lower, so the range takes a round step to 20 instead of 16. The middle of the spread moved from 0.016 to 0.021, and the chance we are alone in the galaxy from 58.8 to 58.0 percent. It was the only range the review moved, and doubling the top of a well-measured piece shifted the answer by less than one point.

The same day every claim on the site still waiting for a source was taken to one: of 27, 11 were confirmed, 8 corrected, 6 removed and 2 are waiting on books ([the changelog](https://github.com/jfredson/quiet-galaxy/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md)).

The record also shows a claim and its corrections. In 2023 and 2025 one team reported a possible sign of life, dimethyl sulfide, in the air of the planet K2-18 b ([the library](/library/)); three later papers in 2025 found no significant evidence for it. No range moved.

On 18 October 2026 [a check of the first automatic reading](/reading/) found it had been too thin: it took three minutes, opened no page, and gave 20 of the 39 records no search of their own. The check found two papers it missed. In one, the Webb telescope showed that two candidates for giant alien structures around stars, called Dyson spheres, are background galaxies. In the other, none of 129 nearby galaxies showed the waste heat such structures would give off. Neither moved a range, and the reading's rules were tightened after the check, starting with the reading of 24 October 2026.

## What to watch for

The result that would move the number most is a second, independent origin of life, one that does not share Earth life's chemistry: in a sample from Mars, in the plumes of Enceladus or Europa, or in the air of a planet around another star. Short of that, a planet with signs of life in its air, or a survey that finds none on hundreds of them, would move the range for whether life starts.

On the calendar, the European Space Agency's Gaia mission makes its [fourth data release on 2 December 2026](https://www.cosmos.esa.int/web/gaia/release), and the record names it as the next thing to read for the share of stars with planets. NASA's [Europa Clipper reaches Jupiter in April 2030](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/europa-clipper/) to study whether Europa's ocean could hold life; its arrival is a milestone, and only what it finds can move a range. The rock at Cheyava Falls on Mars, where the Perseverance rover found what its team called potential signs of life in 2025, has to be examined on Earth to settle it, and the mission to bring it back is unfunded.
