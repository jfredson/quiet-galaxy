Written 2026-10-16 in a Claude Code session, from John's brief. This is the record of where the project started: the question, the name options, the proposed way of building it, and the plan for getting a first version live over the weekend of 2026-10-18 and 2026-10-19. Nothing here is ruled until John says so; the "Decisions" section marks what is proposed and what is settled.

## The question

The sky looks empty. The galaxy is old enough and big enough that, on most ways of counting, someone else should have shown up by now. Nobody has. The puzzle has a name, the Fermi Paradox, after the physicist Enrico Fermi, who asked over lunch in 1950: "Where is everybody?"

The project does for that question what The Belt Equation does for a settled solar system: it takes a question people file under science fiction and breaks it into factors anyone can argue about, with a number at the top that is computed from the pieces and never chosen by hand. The pieces here already have a famous form, the Drake Equation, written by the astronomer Frank Drake in 1961 to organise the first meeting on searching for other civilisations.

Two things the project is for, in John's words: a public, user-friendly place that breaks the equation down factor by factor and keeps its numbers current as discoveries land; and a catalogue of every serious explanation for the silence, each with its own page, so that any explanation can be linked to in a conversation online.

## The headline number

The Drake Equation multiplies seven quantities to get N, the number of civilisations in our galaxy that could be detected right now:

    N = R* × fp × ne × fl × fi × fc × L

In plain words: how many stars are born each year; what share of them have planets; how many of those planets, per star, could support life; on what share of those life actually starts; on what share of those it becomes intelligent; what share of intelligent species make themselves detectable; and how long they stay detectable.

The honest answer to most of these is "somewhere between this and that, across many orders of magnitude", so a single multiplied-out number misleads. The project follows the approach of a 2018 paper by Anders Sandberg, Eric Drexler and Toby Ord ("Dissolving the Fermi Paradox"): each factor carries a range drawn from the published estimates, the equation is run many thousands of times with a random draw from each range, and the result is a spread, not a point. Three numbers come out of that spread and go on the home page:

1. The middle estimate of how many detectable civilisations are in the galaxy right now.
2. The chance we are the only one in the galaxy.
3. The chance we are the only one in the observable universe.

A fourth figure, kept beside them: what the number would be if you take the most optimistic published estimate of every factor, and the most pessimistic. That is the width of the argument, and it is the thing most people have never seen.

Every factor page shows the published estimates it rests on, each with a date and a source, and the range the project currently uses. When a new discovery changes the record for a factor, the range moves, the headline recomputes, and the change is logged with its reason. That is the whole mechanism, and it is the same mechanism The Belt Equation uses for its tree.

## Four kinds of record

The data is the project. Four record types, each one file (or one file per entry) in a plain text format that reads well in a diff, the same TOML layout as The Belt Equation:

1. **Factors** (`data/factors/`). One file per Drake factor: plain name, what it means, the published estimates (value or range, who, when, source, method in one line), the range the project uses now and why, and a revision log. Seven files.
2. **Explanations** (`data/theories/`). One file per explanation for the silence: plain name, the one-paragraph version, the longer version, who proposed it and when, which Drake factor it bears on and in which direction, what evidence would strengthen or weaken it, how seriously it is taken and by whom, related explanations, and the books and films that dramatise it. Each gets its own page at a stable address so it can be linked from anywhere. The explanations sort naturally into three families, which become the catalogue's top-level sections: *they were never there* (the factors before L are small: rare Earth, rare life, rare intelligence), *they are gone* (L is short: self-destruction, the Great Filter ahead of us), and *they are there and we cannot see them* (the equation is fine and the silence is about us: the zoo hypothesis, the dark forest, aestivation, transcension, the simulation, interstellar travel being impractical, we have not looked long or hard enough). About thirty explanations in the first version.
3. **The library** (`data/library/`). Books, films, television, games and videos that treat a factor or an explanation in an interesting way: title, maker, year, kind, which explanation or factor it illustrates, one paragraph on how, and whether it is worth the time. Added by John 2026-10-16. Examples to seed with: *Contact* (Sagan; the equation itself and L), *The Three-Body Problem* (Liu; the dark forest), *Blindsight* (Watts; intelligence without consciousness, fi and fc), *Children of Time* (Tchaikovsky; fl and fi), *Rendezvous with Rama* (Clarke; they pass through), *Arrival* (fc, communication), *Roadside Picnic* (Strugatsky; the picnic hypothesis), *The Expanse* (an ancient civilisation that is gone), *Star Trek*'s Prime Directive (the zoo hypothesis), *2001* and *Interstellar* (watchers), *Revelation Space* (Reynolds; the inhibitors, a berserker), *Manifold: Space* (Baxter), *A Fire Upon the Deep* (Vinge; zones of thought), Isaac Arthur's and Kurzgesagt's video series.
4. **The publications log** (`data/papers/`). An append-only, dated list of the scientific papers and discoveries that bear on the calculation: the founding papers (Drake 1961, Hart 1975, Tipler 1980, Brin 1983 "The Great Silence", Hanson 1998 on the Great Filter, Ward and Brownlee 2000 *Rare Earth*, Ćirković's surveys, Sandberg, Drexler and Ord 2018, Westby and Conselice 2020, Hanson and others 2021 on "grabby aliens", Kipping 2020 on how early life started on Earth), the discovery record (the exoplanet counts and the share of Sun-like stars with Earth-sized planets in the habitable zone, the biosignature claims and retractions around K2-18b in 2023 to 2025, the TRAPPIST-1 atmosphere results, the Breakthrough Listen limits on how many transmitters could be nearby), and from launch onward every new paper the weekly scan turns up. Each entry says which factor or explanation it bears on and whether it moved a range. Added by John 2026-10-16.

The links between the four are what make the site useful: a factor page lists the papers behind its range and the explanations that lean on it; an explanation page lists the papers for and against it and the books and films that dramatise it; a library entry points back at the explanation it illustrates.

## How it is built

Proposed, with confidence and the strongest alternative, per the ground rule The Belt Equation set on 2026-09-08.

1. **Same shape as The Belt Equation: data in TOML, Python standard-library scripts, an Astro static site in `site/`, deployed to Cloudflare Workers static assets, a GitHub Action deploying on every push to main.** High confidence: it is a working pattern with a publish script, a weekly-scan procedure, a deploy workflow and a changelog discipline already debugged, and the first version can copy most of it. Alternative: build the site inside the Sentient Horizons monorepo as a section. Cheaper, but the project is its own thing with its own address, like The Belt Equation.
2. **Join the Sentient Horizons design standard from day one** (`sentient-horizons/design/`, adopted 2026-10-08): copy `deep-field.css` unchanged as `site/src/styles/global.css` with the site's own additions below the marked line, the top bar and footer from `shell.html`, and the parent link in the footer. High confidence, standard practice now that the standard exists. Nothing to decide. The site's public pages follow the standard's "writing for the public" rule: no project shorthand, every idea explained before it is named.
3. **The compute script runs the equation as a spread, not a point** (decision in "The headline number" above). High confidence on the method; it is the published state of the art for this question. Alternative: show the classic single multiplication with Drake's own 1961 values. Keep that as a line on the method page for the history, not as the headline.
4. **A calculator on the equation page**, where the visitor drags seven sliders and sees N change: the one interactive piece, a few hundred lines of plain JavaScript with no framework. High confidence that it belongs in version one: it is the thing most people will play with, and it is the lightest feature on the list. Alternative: defer it. It would cost the site the one moment where a reader feels the width of the argument.
5. **Science tracking as a weekly scheduled cloud task, copied from The Belt Equation's weekly scan** (its Radar, scan plan of 2026-09-19): once a week a fresh session searches for new papers and discoveries against each factor's "what would move this" line, appends what it finds to the publications log with a verdict (nothing relevant; noted but no range moved; moved a range within a cap; flagged for John), reruns the headline, commits to main, and the deploy workflow rebuilds the site. Moderate confidence on the cap and the autonomy, which John ruled for The Belt Equation and would rule again here. Alternative: John files papers by hand. It would work for a month and then stop. Not a weekend item: the first scan runs after launch, once the log has its founding entries.
6. **Critique by GitHub issues with three templates** (a missing explanation; a range that should move, with a source; an error on a page). High confidence, same as The Belt Equation.
7. **Licences from the first commit**: Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 for the data, MIT for the scripts and the site. Settled by the design standard.
8. **No analytics, no comments, no newsletter in version one.** Moderate confidence, same reasoning as The Belt Equation.

Open question for John: whether this is a Python-plus-Astro project like The Belt Equation (proposed) or a Python-writes-HTML project like the AGI Zeitgeist Tracker. The tracker's route is simpler to run but every page component is rebuilt by hand; the Astro route gets the explanation pages, the library and the calculator with far less code. Proposed: Astro.

## Deferred, and why

- **A family tree of explanations drawn as a picture.** Version two; the index by family does the job for now.
- **Per-explanation "what would change my mind" probabilities, scored yearly like The Belt Equation's leaves.** Attractive, but the explanations are not forecasts with resolution dates; think about it after the first annual pass.
- **Analytics, comments, a newsletter.** Only if the posting posture changes.
- **A shared design package across the family.** The design standard decided to copy files, not package them.

## Ground rules carried over

Plain language in everything, and on the public pages the stronger rule from the design standard. Absolute dates. Every change to a range or a record gets a dated changelog line with its source. Facts stated from memory get checked against the web as the work proceeds, and otherwise are marked as unverified for a later session. Commit on main and push at the end of every session.
