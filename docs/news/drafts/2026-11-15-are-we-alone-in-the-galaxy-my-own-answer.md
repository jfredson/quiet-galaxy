The home page of this site carries a number: a 58 percent chance that we are the only civilisation in our galaxy that could be detected right now ([the numbers as they stand](/data/headline.json)). I built the calculation behind that number and I stand by how it is made. But it is not the number I would give if you asked me what I believe. Mine is about 75 percent. This piece says where the difference comes from, and how I divide my belief among [the thirty-two explanations for the silence](/explanations/) that this site keeps.

## What the Calculation Leaves Out

The calculation takes the seven quantities of the [Drake equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drake_equation), gives each one the range that the published estimates support, and multiplies the seven together two hundred thousand times ([how the numbers are worked out](/about/#method)). It answers one question: given what has been published about stars, planets, life and minds, how many civilisations should we expect? It does not use the fact that we have looked and found nobody.

The method follows [a 2018 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.02404) by [Anders Sandberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anders_Sandberg), [Eric Drexler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K._Eric_Drexler) and [Toby Ord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toby_Ord), and that paper shows how much the missing piece matters. Before the silence is counted, the authors put the chance that we are alone in the galaxy at 52 percent. After it is counted, their figure lies somewhere between 53 and 99.6 percent, depending on which kind of evidence is used. The radio searches move the figure by only a few points. Most of the change comes from the fact that nobody has settled our part of the galaxy ([the 2018 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.02404)).

So the 58 percent on [the home page](/) is what the published ranges say before anyone looks at the sky. My 75 percent is what I believe after looking at the sky, and it sits inside the span that the 2018 paper gives.

## The Question I Put to Every Explanation

The universe is about 13.8 billion years old ([We are early](/explanations/we-are-early/)). In 1980 the physicist [Frank Tipler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_J._Tipler) argued that machines able to copy themselves could reach every star in the galaxy within a few million years ([Berserkers](/explanations/berserkers/)). Even if his figure is wrong by a wide margin, the time needed to spread across a galaxy is short beside the time there has been. So the thing to explain is more than the lack of radio signals. Nobody is here, and nobody appears to have been here.

That gives me one question to put to each explanation: does it work only if every civilisation behaves the same way? An explanation that holds even when civilisations differ from one another is strong. An explanation that fails as soon as one civilisation breaks the pattern is weak, because across a whole galaxy and billions of years, one exception is enough to fill the sky.

## How I Split It

The site sorts the explanations into three families: they were never there, they are gone, and they are there and we cannot see them. This is how I divide my belief about the main reason for the silence in our own galaxy.

| The main reason the galaxy is quiet | My share |
|---|---|
| Nobody else arose, because life rarely starts or rarely gets past simple cells | 35 percent |
| Nobody else arose, because intelligence or technology is rare | 15 percent |
| Nobody else arose, because planets like ours are rare, the galaxy was hostile until recently, or we are early | 10 percent |
| Others exist and we have not seen them, because we have searched so little, or because spreading between stars is slow and patchy | 18 percent |
| Others exist and we have not seen them, because they hide, do not care, have become machines, or we live in a simulation | 7 percent |
| Others arose and are gone | 15 percent |

The first three rows add up to 60 percent and the last row adds 15, which is where my 75 percent comes from, against the calculation's 58 ([the numbers as they stand](/data/headline.json)). These shares are judgements. No calculation produced them, and the real answer is probably several of these reasons acting together.

## Why "Never There" Leads

The explanations in this family pass my question easily. If a step on the road to a civilisation is hard, then it is hard everywhere, and no agreement among civilisations is needed.

The step I find hardest to wave away is the one from simple cells to complex ones. Life on Earth stayed single-celled and simple for about two billion years, and every plant, animal and fungus descends from one merger of two simple cells, which happened once in four billion years ([Complex cells are rare](/explanations/complex-cells-are-rare/)). The start of life itself is the least known of the seven quantities: the range this site gives it is fifty powers of ten wide ([the range for whether life starts](/factors/life/)). Those two together get my largest share.

Intelligence and technology get less. Of all the species that have lived on Earth, one built machines ([Rare intelligence](/explanations/rare-intelligence/)). I count that as weaker evidence, because once one species has done it, there is little room left for a second to do the same, so "it happened once" cannot tell "rare" apart from "the first one took the place".

## The Paper That Could Prove Me Wrong

My largest share rests on reading "it happened once, and late" as "it was unlikely". [A 2025 review in Science Advances](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.ads5698) by Daniel Mills and colleagues argues that this reading may be mistaken, and it gives three reasons.

The first is that the planet may have set the pace. By the review's account, a lack of oxygen ruled out complex cells and animals for the first 52 percent of Earth's history, and ruled out humans for the first 91 percent ([the 2025 review](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.ads5698)). On that account complex cells came late because they could not have come sooner.

The second is that "once" may be an accident of what survived. Early repeats of a step could have died out and left nothing behind. The review's example is an amoeba called Paulinella, which took in a light-harvesting bacterium of its own between 90 and 140 million years ago, a step long thought to have happened a single time ([the 2025 review](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.ads5698)).

The third is that the first winner may block the rest. Once one line of living things fills a role, later attempts at the same step are crowded out, so that a step can be easy and still show up only once.

The review does not claim to have settled this. It proposes that there may be no hard steps and names the work that would test the idea. I have left my shares where they were. If those tests come out [the review](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.ads5698)'s way, a good part of my 35 percent has to move to the other two families, and both of them are harder for me to believe.

## The Other Two Families

I give 25 percent to "they are there and we cannot see them", and most of it goes to the dull versions. The astronomer [Jill Tarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jill_Tarter) compared all the searching done so far to dipping one glass of water from the ocean, and a 2018 count put it nearer a large hot tub ([We have not looked long enough](/explanations/not-looked-long-enough/)). That is enough to explain the lack of radio signals. It does not explain why nobody is here. For that I lean on [a 2019 study](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.04450) which found that settlement can spread through a galaxy and still leave some systems empty when settlements do not last for ever ([Expansion stalls](/explanations/expansion-stalls/)).

The versions that depend on a choice get only 7 percent from me. [The zoo hypothesis](/explanations/zoo-hypothesis/) and [the dark forest](/explanations/dark-forest/) each need every civilisation, and every group inside every civilisation, to make the same choice and keep to it for billions of years.

"They are gone" gets 15 percent for a similar reason. For this family to be the whole answer, nearly every civilisation has to end before it spreads, because a single long-lived survivor could spread across the galaxy ([Self-destruction](/explanations/self-destruction/)). I can believe that most civilisations fail. I find it much harder to believe that almost all of them do.

## The Whole Universe

Here my answer falls on the other side of the calculation. The calculation gives a 39 percent chance that we are alone in the whole observable universe, which it counts as 100 billion galaxies like ours ([the numbers as they stand](/data/headline.json)). I would say about 30 percent.

I think we are more likely alone in the galaxy than the calculation says, and less likely alone in the universe. The two do not conflict. A step can be hard enough to leave one galaxy empty and still not be hard enough to leave 100 billion galaxies empty ([how the numbers are worked out](/about/#method)). If that is right, then others exist, most galaxies hold one civilisation or none, and the distances between them are too great for anyone to have noticed anyone else.

## Two Numbers, Kept Apart

The 58 percent and the 75 percent answer different questions. The first is what the published ranges give when they are multiplied together ([how the numbers are worked out](/about/#method)). The second is what one person makes of those ranges and of the empty sky. The number on the home page stays the first kind, and it changes only when a published finding changes one of the seven ranges. Mine can be wrong in ways the calculation cannot, and [the 2025 review in Science Advances](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.ads5698) is the clearest account I have read of how.
