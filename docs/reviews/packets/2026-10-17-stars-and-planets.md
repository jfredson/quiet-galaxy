## Factor 1 of 7: How many stars are born in the galaxy each year (R*)
Unit: stars per year.

### What it is
The rate at which the Milky Way makes new stars, averaged over the time it takes a star to
grow a civilisation. It is the best-measured factor in the equation: the galaxy's output of
new stars is read from the glow of young stars and the gas they heat, and comes to roughly one
and a half to two solar masses a year, which is three or four stars a year once the many small
stars are counted. The one real argument is over which rate to use: today's, or the higher
average over the galaxy's whole life, since the stars that could host a civilisation today
were mostly born billions of years ago.

### What would move this
A new measurement of the Milky Way's present star-formation rate, or of its star-formation history, that falls outside one to ten stars a year.

### The published estimates the record lists
1. **Frank Drake and the Green Bank meeting, 1961: 1.** One star a year on average over the life of the galaxy, which the meeting regarded as conservative.
   Source: Green Bank meeting of November 1961, as recorded in Drake and Sobel, Is Anyone Out There? (Delacorte, 1992), pages 55 to 62; the meeting's record is the founding entry of the publications log (drake-1961)

   Source: Robitaille and Whitney, 'The present-day star formation rate of the Milky Way determined from Spitzer-detected young stellar objects', Astrophysical Journal Letters 710, L11 (2010)
   Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/1001.3672

### The range the site uses now
Shape: log-uniform. Low: 1. High: 10. Status: first-pass. Set on 2026-10-16.

The reason the record gives:

Narrower than the 2018 paper's 1 to 100: the top of their span is far above any measurement of
this galaxy. Measurements of today's rate (Robitaille and Whitney 2010, Licquia and Newman
2015) cluster between 1.4 and 3.7 stars a year. The range reaches up to 10 because the stars
that could host a civilisation today were mostly born long ago, when the galaxy made stars
faster: spreading its roughly six times ten to the tenth solar masses of stars over its ten
billion years gives an average of about six solar masses, or ten stars, a year. Drake's 1961
value of one star a year is the floor. Evenly spread across powers of ten, the same shape the
2018 paper used, because the question is which power of ten, not which digit.

## Factor 2 of 7: The share of stars that have planets (fp)
Unit: fraction.

### What it is
Of all the stars in the galaxy, what fraction have at least one planet. In 1961 this was
a guess; since the first exoplanet discoveries in the 1990s and the Kepler mission of 2009
to 2018 it has become one of the two best-measured factors in the equation. The current
reading is that planets are the rule, not the exception: every method that counts them,
whether by the dip in a star's light as a planet crosses it, by the wobble of the star, or by
the bending of light from a background star, finds planets around most or all stars.

### What would move this
A survey that counts planets around a large, well-understood sample of stars, including the dim red dwarfs that make up most of the galaxy, and reports the share of stars with none.

### The published estimates the record lists
1. **Frank Drake and the Green Bank meeting, 1961: 0.2 to 0.5.** Educated guess before any planet outside the solar system had been found.
   Source: Green Bank meeting of November 1961, as recorded in Drake and Sobel, Is Anyone Out There? (Delacorte, 1992), pages 55 to 62, and in Drake, Vakoch and Dowd, The Drake Equation: Estimating the Prevalence of Extraterrestrial Life through the Ages (Cambridge University Press, 2015); the meeting's record is the founding entry of the publications log (drake-1961)

2. **Arnaud Cassan and colleagues, 2012-01-12: 0.62 to 1.** Gravitational microlensing over six years of survey data; 62% of stars found to host a planet of a few Earth masses at 0.5 to 10 times the Earth-Sun distance, and the planets per star add to more than one, so the share of stars with any planet lies between the two.
   Source: Cassan et al., 'One or more bound planets per Milky Way star from microlensing observations', Nature 481, 167–169 (2012)
   Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/1202.0903

3. **Carl Sagan, 1980: 0.33.** A round guess, one third, in a worked example of the equation written for a general audience before any exoplanet was known.
   Source: Sagan, Cosmos (Random House, 1980), chapter 12, 'Encyclopaedia Galactica'

4. **Michel Mayor and colleagues, 2011-09: 0.5 to 1.** Eight years of measuring the wobble of 822 Sun-like stars (376 with the HARPS instrument, the rest from an earlier survey with the CORALIE instrument); 51 to 57 percent were found to host at least one planet with an orbit shorter than 100 days (50.6 plus or minus 7.4 percent counting published planets, 57.1 plus or minus 8.0 percent with candidates), after correcting for the planets missed. Planets on longer orbits were not counted, so the share with any planet is higher.
   Source: Mayor et al., 'The HARPS search for southern extra-solar planets XXXIV. Occurrence, mass distribution and orbital properties of super-Earths and Neptune-mass planets', arXiv:1109.2497 (2011)
   Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/1109.2497

5. **Courtney Dressing and David Charbonneau, 2015-07: 0.9 to 1.** Kepler's four-year count of planets crossing red dwarf stars, corrected for the ones it would have missed: 2.5 plus or minus 0.2 planets of one to four Earth radii per red dwarf with orbits under 200 days. Red dwarfs are three quarters of all stars, and with that many planets per star almost every one has at least one; the lower figure here allows for planets bunching around some stars.
   Source: Dressing and Charbonneau, 'The occurrence of potentially habitable planets orbiting M dwarfs estimated from the full Kepler dataset and an empirical measurement of the detection sensitivity', Astrophysical Journal 807, 45 (2015)
   Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/1501.01623

6. **Anders Sandberg, Eric Drexler and Toby Ord, 2018-06: 0.1 to 1.** A span evenly spread across its powers of ten, chosen to cover the published argument; the paper notes that 'different methods are converging on fp ≈ 1'.
   Source: Sandberg, Drexler and Ord, 'Dissolving the Fermi Paradox', arXiv:1806.02404 (2018), Table 1 (sandberg-2018)
   Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.02404

## Factor 3 of 7: Planets per star that could support life (ne)
Unit: planets per star.

### What it is
For a star with planets, how many of them could support life: in practice, how many rocky
planets of roughly Earth's size orbit where water could be liquid on the surface. The Kepler
mission turned this from a guess into a measurement for Sun-like stars and for red dwarfs,
with the answer coming out between a tenth and one such planet per star. What the surveys
cannot count is whether such a planet also has the other things Earth has, such as a large
moon, moving continents and a quiet neighbourhood, which is where the argument over this
factor now lives.

### What would move this
A new count of rocky planets in the habitable zone per star from Kepler, TESS, Gaia or PLATO data, or a measurement of how often such planets keep an atmosphere and liquid water (the TRAPPIST-1 and K2-18b results bear on this).

### The published estimates the record lists
1. **Frank Drake and the Green Bank meeting, 1961: 1 to 5.** Educated guess from the solar system, where Venus, Earth and Mars all orbit near the zone where water can be liquid.
   Source: Green Bank meeting of November 1961, as recorded in Drake and Sobel, Is Anyone Out There? (Delacorte, 1992), pages 55 to 62 (drake-1961)

2. **Carl Sagan, 1980: 2.** A round guess in a worked example of the equation for a general audience, before any exoplanet was known.
   Source: Sagan, Cosmos (Random House, 1980), chapter 12, 'Encyclopaedia Galactica'

3. **Peter Ward and Donald Brownlee, 2000: 1e-5 to 0.01.** Said that, after allowing for how common planets are and for the location and lifetime of the habitable zone, the ordinary Drake equation suggests that only between 1% and 0.001% of all stars might have planets with habitats similar to Earth's, and that even these numbers are probably too high. They also ruled out red dwarfs as hosts. Their further extras (a large moon, plate tectonics, few mass extinctions) come after this passage and are not counted in the figure. Taken here as planets per star, since nearly every star has planets.
   Source: Ward and Brownlee, Rare Earth: Why Complex Life Is Uncommon in the Universe (Copernicus, 2000), pages 269 to 270, chapter 'Assessing the Odds' (ward-2000)

4. **Erik Petigura, Andrew Howard and Geoffrey Marcy, 2013-11: 0.14 to 0.3.** Kepler's count of planets crossing 42,000 Sun-like stars, corrected for the planets it would have missed by injecting fake ones into the data: 22 plus or minus 8 percent of Sun-like stars have a planet of one to two Earth radii receiving between a quarter and four times the light Earth gets (the paper's abstract gives the directly measured 11 plus or minus 4 percent for one to four times the light Earth gets; the 22 percent extends that down to a quarter, assuming equal numbers of planets in each step of the logarithm of the light received).
   Source: Petigura, Howard and Marcy, 'Prevalence of Earth-size planets orbiting Sun-like stars', Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 110, 19273–19278 (2013)
   Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/1311.6806

5. **Courtney Dressing and David Charbonneau, 2015-07: 0.09 to 0.33.** Kepler's count of planets crossing red dwarf stars, the most common kind, corrected for the ones it would have missed: 0.16 (between 0.09 and 0.33) Earth-size planets per red dwarf in the strictly defined habitable zone, plus another 0.12 planets somewhat larger than Earth; the wider habitable zone that allows for early Venus and early Mars gives 0.24 and 0.21.
   Source: Dressing and Charbonneau, 'The occurrence of potentially habitable planets orbiting M dwarfs estimated from the full Kepler dataset and an empirical measurement of the detection sensitivity', Astrophysical Journal 807, 45 (2015)
   Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/1501.01623

6. **Steve Bryson and 81 colleagues (the Kepler team), 2020-11: 0.16 to 1.5.** The Kepler mission's final planet catalogue combined with Gaia's measurements of the stars, corrected for what the catalogue missed and for false detections: between 0.37 (0.16 to 0.85) and 0.60 (0.24 to 1.50) planets of half to one and a half Earth radii per Sun-like star in the strictly defined habitable zone, the two figures reflecting two ways of extrapolating to orbits Kepler could not see; the generous habitable zone gives 0.58 to 0.88. The nearest such planet is estimated to be about 20 light-years away.
   Source: Bryson et al., 'The occurrence of rocky habitable zone planets around solar-like stars from Kepler data', Astronomical Journal 161, 36 (2021); posted 2020-10-28 (bryson-2020)
   Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.14812

7. **Anders Sandberg, Eric Drexler and Toby Ord, 2018-06: 0.1 to 1.** A span evenly spread across its powers of ten, taking an Earth-like planet to mean a rocky planet in the habitable zone; the paper notes that the published argument runs from below one in a trillion (the Rare Earth case) to more than one (counting icy moons).
   Source: Sandberg, Drexler and Ord, 'Dissolving the Fermi Paradox', arXiv:1806.02404 (2018), Table 1 (sandberg-2018)
   Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.02404

The reason the record gives:

The Kepler counts (Petigura 2013, Dressing and Charbonneau 2015, Bryson 2020) put rocky
planets in the habitable zone at between a tenth and one per star for both Sun-like stars and
red dwarfs, with the most careful study's error bars reaching one and a half. The top of the

atmosphere results of 2023 to 2025 show rocky planets around red dwarfs can lose their air.
It stops at 0.01 rather than following Ward and Brownlee down to one in a hundred thousand
because their extras are an argument, not a count, and the explanation page carries it. Evenly
spread across powers of ten, as in the 2018 paper.

# From the method document: what the computation does

## What the computation does

The Drake Equation multiplies seven quantities: how many stars are born each year, the share with planets, the planets per star that could support life, the share of those where life starts, the share of those where it becomes intelligent, the share of intelligent species that are detectable, and how long they stay detectable. Each factor's record in `data/factors/` carries a range and a shape. One run draws one value from every range and multiplies the seven. The equation is played out two hundred thousand times, and the headline numbers are read off the spread of results:

1. **The middle estimate** of detectable civilisations in the galaxy: the median of the runs. Not the mean, which the rare very optimistic runs pull up to millions (the 2018 paper's mean was 27 million against a median of 0.32).
2. **The chance we are alone in the galaxy**: the share of runs in which the result came out below one. (The exact chance that a galaxy with an expected N civilisations has none is e to the minus N; the 2018 paper's footnote 3 shows the difference from the share below one is about one percentage point, and we follow them in using the share.)
3. **The chance we are alone in the observable universe**: the share of runs in which the result came out below one per the number of Milky-Way-sized galaxies' worth of stars we can see.
4. **The width of the argument**: the result when every factor takes its most optimistic published estimate at once, and when every factor takes its most pessimistic. Computed from the estimates in the records, as the kickoff defines it; the same figure from the ranges' ends is reported beside it.

Three shapes are allowed by the record layout: `log-uniform` (every power of ten between the ends is equally likely, right for a quantity whose uncertainty is "which power of ten"), `uniform` (every value equally likely), and `log-normal-rate`, the 2018 paper's model for the chance life starts (item 7 below), which has no ends: the number of times life starts on a habitable planet over its life is drawn from a bell curve in its powers of ten, and the chance is one minus e to the minus that number. Six ranges are log-uniform; the chance life starts uses the third shape since John's ruling of 2026-10-16.

Where a low and a high are needed for a range with no ends (the band on the calculator, the width from the ranges' ends), the pessimistic end is taken as the chance one spread below the centre (about one in ten to the fifty for the paper's shape) and the optimistic end as 1; where a starting value is needed (the calculator's slider), the chance at the centre, 0.63. These are display conveniences, written in `scripts/compute.py` as `range_ends` and `range_middle`.
