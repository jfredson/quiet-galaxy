# The records in this packet: The share of planets that could support life on which life actually starts, The share of planets with life on which it becomes intelligent

## Factor 4 of 7: The share of planets that could support life on which life actually starts (fl)
Unit: fraction.

### What it is
Given a planet where water is liquid and the chemistry is right, how likely is it that life
gets going at all. We know of exactly one case, and on that planet life appeared early, within
a few hundred million years of the surface becoming habitable, which optimists read as a sign
it is easy. Pessimists answer that we would be here to notice however unlikely it was, and that
the step from chemistry to a self-copying molecule may be so improbable that it happens once per
galaxy or once per universe. No measurement exists, and this is the factor on which the whole
answer most depends. A second, independent origin of life anywhere (Mars, an icy moon, an
exoplanet atmosphere) would settle it.

### What would move this
Evidence of life, or of its chemical signatures, anywhere beyond Earth (Mars samples, Enceladus or Europa plumes, exoplanet atmospheres such as K2-18b), or a laboratory demonstration of how a self-copying molecule arises and how improbable the step is.

### The record's notes
Two lines of argument carry no number and so are not estimates but matter for the range. On
the pessimistic side, Koonin (2007) and Totani (2020) argue from the chemistry that a self-copying
system is so unlikely to form by chance that it would not start even once in the observable
universe (Koonin puts the chance below one in ten to the 1,018 for a coupled copying-and-translating
system in one observable region, a deliberately generous upper bound; Totani finds random chemistry in
the observable universe's ten to the 22 stars makes RNA chains of about 32 units, against the 40 to 100
needed); both then conclude that life can still arise somewhere in a far larger, unobservable
universe (Koonin's infinite multiverse; Totani's inflated universe of more than ten to the 100
stars). Their figures are so small that no computation can use them, and the range
treats 'once per observable universe' as the floor they imply. On the optimistic side, Kipping's
2020 Bayesian analysis of when life and intelligence appeared on Earth (kipping-2020) finds that
life starting quickly is at least 2.8 times more likely than life starting slowly, 8.7 times if
disputed 4.1-billion-year-old carbon traces are accepted, while cautioning that the analysis
concerns Earth and says nothing about planets unlike it. Spiegel and Turner (2012) had argued the
opposite from the same evidence: that with one example and the fact that we must exist to ask,
the early start tells us little. The 'hard steps' argument (Carter 1983; Hanson 1998 on the Great
Filter, hanson-1998; Hanson and colleagues 2021, hanson-2021-grabby, who put the number of hard
steps at about six) says that whichever steps between chemistry and a spacefaring species are
the hard ones, Earth's history fits a world that got lucky several times. Checked 2026-10-16
against the papers: Sagan's one third, Westby and Conselice's assumption that this factor is 1
(their section 2.2), and Spiegel and Turner's conclusion (PNAS 109, 395; the choice of prior
dominates, and the evidence is consistent with an arbitrarily low chance) are borne out; the
Koonin and Totani figures are as described above, which differs from the first draft. The range's
reason below, written earlier, says Kipping's 'three-to-one' (the paper gives 2.8 to 1) and
that Koonin and Totani place the chance 'without giving a usable number'; neither changes the
range, but both should be revisited at the outside review. Written 2026-10-16.

- **David Kipping (2020-05-18), "An objective Bayesian analysis of life's early start and our late arrival", Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 117, 11995–12003 (2020).** Earth's life started quickly, within a few hundred million years of the planet becoming

two dates say about how likely each step is on an Earth-like planet, taking care to
assume as little as possible beforehand. He finds odds of at least about 2.8 to 1 that life

- **David S. Spiegel, Edwin L. Turner (2012-01-10), "Bayesian analysis of the astrobiological implications of life's early emergence on Earth", Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 109, 395–400 (2012).** The common argument that life must be easy because it started so soon on Earth is
weaker than it looks, the authors show, because we could only be here to notice if life
had started early enough to leave time for us to evolve. Once that selection is taken
into account, the early start is consistent with life being common and also with life
being very rare, and which you conclude depends mostly on what you believed before
looking. Their conclusion is that one planet's history cannot tell us the chance life
starts elsewhere, and that finding a second, independent origin of life, on Mars or
Europa, is the only thing that would.
- **Peter D. Ward, Donald Brownlee (2000-01), "Rare Earth: Why Complex Life Is Uncommon in the Universe", Copernicus Books (Springer), New York, 2000, 333 pages.** A palaeontologist and an astronomer argue that simple life may be common but that animals
are not, because Earth's history depended on a long list of conditions that rarely come
together: the right kind of star in the right part of the galaxy, a large moon that
steadies the tilt, Jupiter sweeping up comets, plate tectonics recycling carbon, a
magnetic field, and an unusually quiet several hundred million years in which complex
cells could arise. They split the Drake Equation's habitable-planets factor into many
smaller ones and conclude that the product is tiny. The book named the position, and
every later argument about how rare Earth-like conditions are starts by agreeing or
disagreeing with its list.
- **Robin Hanson (1998-09-15), "The Great Filter – Are We Almost Past It?", Essay published on the author's website, George Mason University, 1998 (first version 1996).** Hanson's essay gives the modern framing. Somewhere between dead matter and a civilisation
that spreads across the galaxy there must be at least one step that almost nothing gets
past, because if every step were easy the galaxy would be visibly full. He calls that
step the Great Filter and asks the only question that matters for us: is it behind us or
ahead? If life, or complex cells, or intelligence is the hard step, we are through it
and the silence is good news. If the hard step is surviving technology, then every
species gets this far and then stops, and the silence is a warning. He draws the
practical conclusion that finding simple life on Mars or Europa would be bad news,
because it would move the filter forward.
- **Brandon Carter (1983-12-20), "The anthropic principle and its implications for biological evolution", Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London A 310, 347–363 (1983).** Carter notices that intelligence on Earth took about four billion years to appear, and
that the Sun will only stay fit for life for something like five to ten billion years in
all. If intelligence usually took far less time than a star's life, we would expect it to
have appeared early, not with the clock nearly run out; if it usually took far more, we
would not be here at all. The near-match, he argues, is what you would see if the
expected time for intelligence to evolve is much longer than a star's lifetime, and we are
a lucky exception. From the timing he estimates that the path from simple life to
observers has only one or two very improbable steps, and that intelligent life is
correspondingly rare. This is the argument behind every 'hard steps' calculation since.

Cameron (Benjamin, 1963), 287–293.** Drake wrote the equation on a blackboard as the agenda for a three-day meeting of about ten
people: the number of detectable civilisations in the galaxy equals the rate at which
suitable stars form, times the share with planets, times the habitable planets per such
star, times the share on which life starts, times the share where it becomes intelligent,
times the share that become detectable, times how long they stay so. The meeting's own
values, as later recounted, were about 1 to 10 stars a year, 0.2 to 0.5, 1 to 5, 1, 1, 0.1
to 0.2, and a lifetime of 1,000 to 100,000,000 years, which made the number of
civilisations roughly equal to the lifetime in years: somewhere between 1,000 and 100
million. The equation was never meant as a calculation; it was a way to organise an
argument, and it has done that job for sixty-five years.

## Factor 5 of 7: The share of planets with life on which it becomes intelligent (fi)
Unit: fraction.

### What it is
Given a planet with life, how likely it is that some of that life eventually becomes
intelligent in the sense that matters here: able to build technology. On Earth it took about
four billion years, most of it spent as single cells, and happened in one lineage out of
billions of species, which the biologist Ernst Mayr took as proof that it is a fluke. Others
read the steady rise in the complexity of life as a sign that intelligence is close to
inevitable given time. There is no second example, and the only new evidence is statistical:
reasoning from how late in Earth's habitable life we arrived.

### What would move this
A second example of complex or intelligent life anywhere, a change in the fossil record's timeline for the major steps (complex cells, animals, brains), or a published analysis that puts a new number on how often intelligence follows from life.

### The published estimates the record lists
1. **Frank Drake and the Green Bank meeting, 1961: 1.** Assumed: the meeting took it that intelligence follows from life given enough time.
   Source: Green Bank meeting of November 1961, as recorded in Drake and Sobel, Is Anyone Out There? (Delacorte, 1992), pages 55 to 62 (drake-1961)

A count of species, not of planets, which is why it is recorded but not used for the range's floor.
   Source: Mayr, 'Can SETI Succeed? # From the method document: what the computation does

## What the computation does

The Drake Equation multiplies seven quantities: how many stars are born each year, the share with planets, the planets per star that could support life, the share of those where life starts, the share of those where it becomes intelligent, the share of intelligent species that are detectable, and how long they stay detectable. Each factor's record in `data/factors/` carries a range and a shape. One run draws one value from every range and multiplies the seven. The equation is played out two hundred thousand times, and the headline numbers are read off the spread of results:

1. **The middle estimate** of detectable civilisations in the galaxy: the median of the runs. Not the mean, which the rare very optimistic runs pull up to millions (the 2018 paper's mean was 27 million against a median of 0.32).
2. **The chance we are alone in the galaxy**: the share of runs in which the result came out below one. (The exact chance that a galaxy with an expected N civilisations has none is e to the minus N; the 2018 paper's footnote 3 shows the difference from the share below one is about one percentage point, and we follow them in using the share.)
3. **The chance we are alone in the observable universe**: the share of runs in which the result came out below one per the number of Milky-Way-sized galaxies' worth of stars we can see.
4. **The width of the argument**: the result when every factor takes its most optimistic published estimate at once, and when every factor takes its most pessimistic. Computed from the estimates in the records, as the kickoff defines it; the same figure from the ranges' ends is reported beside it.

Three shapes are allowed by the record layout: `log-uniform` (every power of ten between the ends is equally likely, right for a quantity whose uncertainty is "which power of ten"), `uniform` (every value equally likely), and `log-normal-rate`, the 2018 paper's model for the chance life starts (item 7 below), which has no ends: the number of times life starts on a habitable planet over its life is drawn from a bell curve in its powers of ten, and the chance is one minus e to the minus that number. Six ranges are log-uniform; the chance life starts uses the third shape since John's ruling of 2026-10-16.

Where a low and a high are needed for a range with no ends (the band on the calculator, the width from the ranges' ends), the pessimistic end is taken as the chance one spread below the centre (about one in ten to the fifty for the paper's shape) and the optimistic end as 1; where a starting value is needed (the calculator's slider), the chance at the centre, 0.63. These are display conveniences, written in `scripts/compute.py` as `range_ends` and `range_middle`.
