# The records in this packet: The share of intelligent species that make themselves detectable, How long a civilisation stays detectable, in years

## Factor 6 of 7: The share of intelligent species that make themselves detectable (fc)
Unit: fraction.

### What it is
Given an intelligent species, how likely it is to do something we could notice from here:
leak or send radio, light up its planet, change its atmosphere, build things in space. Drake
had deliberate radio messages in mind; the modern reading is broader, since our own radar,
television and city lights have made Earth detectable to a neighbour with a good enough
telescope without our choosing it. The factor also covers the choice not to be found, which is
where several of the explanations for the silence live. Nothing here is measured; every figure
is an assumption.

### What would move this
A survey result that bounds how many transmitters or technological signatures could be nearby (the Breakthrough Listen limits), or a published argument with a number on how often technological species stay quiet or go dark.

   Source: Green Bank meeting of November 1961, as recorded in Drake and Sobel, Is Anyone Out There? (Delacorte, 1992), pages 55 to 62 (drake-1961)

2. **Anders Sandberg, Eric Drexler and Toby Ord, 2018-06: 0.01 to 1.** A span evenly spread across its powers of ten, from the published estimates of 0.01 to 1, with the paper's remark that a broader range is clearly intended by some authors.
   Source: Sandberg, Drexler and Ord, 'Dissolving the Fermi Paradox', arXiv:1806.02404 (2018), Table 1 (sandberg-2018)

### The record's notes

- **Ian A. can host such heat at the thresholds the sample can test. For a civilisation using a galaxy's worth

- **Louisa A. Mason, Michael A. Garrett, Andrew P. V. Siemion (2025-11-25), "Simulating the Stellar Bycatch: Constraining the Prevalence of Extraterrestrial Transmitters within Radio SETI Surveys", arXiv:2511.20231 (astro-ph.IM, with astro-ph.GA), 8 pages.** Counts every star that fell inside a radio telescope's beam during a large Breakthrough Listen

- **Michael Garrett (2025-09-28), "Blink and you'll miss it: How Technological Acceleration Shrinks SETI's Narrow Detection Window", arXiv:2509.23632 (astro-ph.IM), 15 pages; accepted by Acta Astronautica.** Proposes that civilisations may advance so fast after reaching our stage that the phase in which
their technology is detectable to us lasts mere decades, so the overlap between their signals and
our instruments is tiny even if they are common and long-lived. On this view the silence says
nothing about how many there are. The paper argues for searches that do not assume a technology:
persistent large-scale traces such as waste heat and broadband leakage, and anomaly detection across

- **Robin Hanson, Daniel Martin, Calvin McCarter and others (2021-12), "If Loud Aliens Explain Human Earliness, Quiet Aliens Are Also Rare", The Astrophysical Journal 922, 182 (2021); arXiv:2102.01522.** The puzzle the paper starts from is that humans seem to have arrived early: most of the
universe's habitable time lies ahead, around small stars that will burn for trillions of
years, so an observer picked at random should expect to appear much later than 13.8
billion years in. The authors' explanation is that some civilisations expand ('grabby'
ones), fill space at a good fraction of light speed, and make their volume uninhabitable
for newcomers, so anyone who appears at all has to appear before the grabbers arrive. A
three-parameter model fit to our own date gives their picture of the universe: grabby
civilisations already control about 40 to 50 percent of the universe's volume, each will
eventually control roughly 100,000 to 30 million galaxies, and our descendants could meet
them in something like 200 million to 2 billion years; the volumes they control would
look very large in our sky. The title's point is that if this is
right, quiet civilisations that never expand cannot be much more common than loud ones.
- **Vishal Gajjar, Karen I. Perez, Andrew P. V. Siemion and others (2021-07), "The Breakthrough Listen Search for Intelligent Life Near the Galactic Center. I.", The Astronomical Journal 162, 33 (2021).** The centre of the galaxy packs the most stars into the smallest patch of sky, so a
single pointing covers tens of millions of them, and a civilisation wanting to be heard
across the galaxy might reasonably signal from or toward it. Breakthrough Listen observed
the central region for about 18 hours (7.0 with Parkes and 11.2 with Green Bank) between
1 and 8 gigahertz, and found no artificial signals, though it did pick up bursts from a
known magnetar. The limits are coarse because the stars are so far away: only a
transmitter far more powerful than anything humans have built would have been seen. The
paper lays out the plan for a deeper survey of the region and is the first of a series.
- **Bart S. Wlodarczyk-Sroka, Michael A. Garrett, Andrew P. V. Siemion (2020-09), "Extending the Breakthrough Listen nearby star survey to other stellar objects in the field", Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society 498, 5720–5729 (2020).** A radio telescope pointed at one star also hears every other star in its beam. Using the
Gaia catalogue of star positions and distances, the authors count how many stars were in
the beams of price-2020's 1,327 target stars: 288,315, out to thousands of light
years, most of them far more distant than the targets. Because a transmitter at a greater
distance has to be more powerful to be heard, the limits are weaker for those stars, but
there are two hundred times more of them. The paper's result is that no more than about
0.04 percent of stars within about 650 light years (200 parsecs) host a transmitter of at
least 2.5 x 10^14 watts that is on most of the time, the first limit of its kind on a sample that large, and a reminder that the
sky has been searched less than it feels.
- **Danny C. Price, J. Emilio Enriquez, Bryan Brzycki and others (2020-02-05), "The Breakthrough Listen Search for Intelligent Life: Observations of 1327 Nearby Stars over 1.10–3.45 GHz", The Astronomical Journal 159, 86 (2020).** Breakthrough Listen, the privately funded ten-year radio search begun in 2016, reports
its first large survey: 1,327 stars within about 160 light years, observed with the
Green Bank and Parkes telescopes across a wide band of radio frequencies, with no signal
found that could not be traced to human interference. The authors turn the silence into
a limit: at 95 percent confidence, fewer than about 0.45 percent of the stars watched with Green
Bank host a narrow-band transmitter above about 2 trillion watts, and fewer than about 2
percent on the Parkes data above about 9 trillion watts (all the stars lie within 50 parsecs). The limits apply only to the narrow
kind of signal searched for, as the paper says, and to the moments the telescope was
looking.
- **Jason T. Wright, Shubham Kanodia, Emily Lubar (2018-11), "How Much SETI Has Been Done? Finding Needles in the n-dimensional Cosmic Haystack", The Astronomical Journal 156, 260 (2018).** The authors define the space a radio search has to cover (where on the sky, how far,
which frequencies, how strong, how often, when, and how the signal is shaped) as a
many-dimensional haystack, and add up how much of it every search to date had looked
through. The total comes to about six parts in 10 to the power 18 of the plausible
volume, which they compare to somewhere between a large hot tub and a small swimming pool
of water drawn from all the Earth's oceans; Jill Tarter's earlier comparison was a single glass. The paper is the standard
answer to 'we have searched and found nothing': in a precise sense, we have hardly
searched at all, and the silence so far is a weak constraint on the number of
civilisations.
- **Anders Sandberg, Eric Drexler, Toby Ord (2018-06-06), "Dissolving the Fermi Paradox", arXiv:1806.02404 (physics.pop-ph), Future of Humanity Institute, Oxford; submitted to Proceedings of the Royal Society A.** Run with a range for each factor instead of a single value, the Drake Equation gives a
median of 0.32 detectable civilisations in the galaxy, a 52% chance that we are alone in
the galaxy and a 38% chance that we are alone in the observable universe (their Table 2,
'no update' row). Taking the silence itself into account raises those to between 53% and
99.6%, and between 39% and 85%, depending on what the silence is taken to show. The paper's
point is that the paradox comes from treating very uncertain factors as if they were known.
- **Adam Frank, Woodruff T. Sullivan III (2016-05), "A New Empirical Constraint on the Prevalence of Technological Species in the Universe", Astrobiology 16, 359–362 (2016).** The authors drop the lifetime factor and ask a different question: not how many
civilisations exist now, but how many have ever existed. With the exoplanet record
fixing the first three factors, the product of the remaining biological ones would have
to be below about 2.5 in 10 to the power 24 for humanity to be the only technological
species the observable universe has ever produced, and below about 1.7 in 10 to the
power 11 for us to be the only one in the Milky Way's history. In plain terms, unless
the chance of a habitable planet producing a technological species is almost
unimaginably small, we are not the first. The paper does not say anyone is out there
now; it says the pessimists need a very strong claim to say nobody ever was.
- **Anders Sandberg, Stuart Armstrong, Milan M. Ćirković (2016), "That is not dead which can eternal lie: the aestivation hypothesis for resolving Fermi's paradox", Journal of the British Interplanetary Society 69, 406–415 (2016); arXiv:1705.03394 (2017).** Computation costs energy, and the cost of erasing a bit of information falls as the
surroundings get colder. The universe is still warm from its birth and will cool for
trillions of years, so a civilisation that cares most about how much thinking it can do
in total would gain about a factor of 10 to the power 30 by waiting: do nothing now,
sleep through the hot early universe, and wake when the cosmos is cold. The authors
propose that advanced civilisations may be doing exactly that, dormant but present, which
would explain why we see neither them nor their works. They note it predicts that
someone should be guarding resources against waste, and that we see no sign of such
guarding, which is the main argument against it.
- **Robert H. Gray (2015-03), "The Fermi Paradox Is Neither Fermi's Nor a Paradox", Astrobiology 15, 195–199 (2015).** A history paper that checks the story. Going back to the recollections of the people at
the 1950 Los Alamos lunch where Fermi asked 'where is everybody?', Gray finds that Fermi
was asking about interstellar travel, not doubting that other civilisations exist, and
that the argument now called the Fermi paradox (they would be here by now, so they do not
exist) was made by Hart in 1975 and Tipler in 1980. He also argues it is not a paradox
but an argument with disputable premises, mainly that every civilisation would colonise
and that colonisation would be fast and complete. The paper is the reason this project
calls the thing the silence rather than the paradox where it can.
- **Stuart Armstrong, Anders Sandberg (2013-08), "Eternity in six hours: Intergalactic spreading of intelligent life and sharpening the Fermi paradox", Acta Astronautica 89, 1–13 (2013).** Using only physics and engineering we already understand in outline (dismantling the
planet Mercury for materials, solar-powered launchers, self-copying probes slowed by
magnetic sails), the authors show that a single civilisation could launch probes to
every galaxy within reach, millions of them, and that the whole launch would take about
six hours of the Sun's energy output. The galaxy is therefore not the right scale for
the paradox: if even one civilisation in a billion galaxies had ever chosen to expand,
its probes should be here. That makes explanations that rely on every civilisation
independently choosing not to spread much harder to hold, and pushes the weight onto
explanations where civilisations are very rare or do not last.
- **John M. Smart (2012-09), "The transcension hypothesis: Sufficiently advanced civilizations invariably leave our universe, and implications for METI and SETI", Acta Astronautica 78, 55–68 (2012).** Smart proposes that technological civilisations do not expand outward but inward: each
step of progress packs more computation into less space, time, energy and matter, and
the end of that road is something like a black hole, where a civilisation would find the
densest possible computing and, perhaps, a way out of this universe altogether. If that
is the universal pattern, every advanced civilisation disappears from view at roughly
the same stage, shortly after ours, and the galaxy's silence is what a developmental law
looks like from outside. He draws the practical conclusion that sending messages is
pointless and that the search should look for the brief signatures of civilisations in
transition.
- **Milan M. Ćirković (2009-06), "Fermi's Paradox: The Last Challenge for Copernicanism?", Serbian Astronomical Journal 178, 1–20 (2009); arXiv:0907.3432.** The most careful modern survey of the explanations, by the astronomer who later wrote
the book-length version (The Great Silence, Oxford University Press, 2018). Ćirković
argues that the paradox has grown sharper, not weaker, since 1975, because we now know
that planets are common, that the galaxy's habitable stars are mostly older than the Sun,
and that spreading through it is cheap on cosmic timescales. He sorts the explanations
into four families: solipsist (they are here and we are wrong about what we see), rare
Earth (we are alone or nearly), neocatastrophic (civilisations keep being reset by
disasters, natural or self-made), and logistic (there are limits on expansion or
communication we do not yet understand). He rates most of the familiar explanations
poorly and argues the catastrophic and logistic families deserve more work.
- **Jacob D. Haqq-Misra, Seth D. Baum (2009), "The Sustainability Solution to the Fermi Paradox", Journal of the British Interplanetary Society 62, 47–51 (2009); arXiv:0906.0568.** Hart's argument assumes that a civilisation keeps growing exponentially for millions of
years. The authors point out that nothing on Earth has ever done that: fast-growing
populations and economies hit limits and either collapse or level off. If the same holds
elsewhere, civilisations that grow fast enough to fill a galaxy would be exactly the ones
that burn out first, and the ones that last would be those that grew slowly or stopped,
and therefore never spread far. The galaxy could then hold many old, stable, small
civilisations and no empire. The paper's practical point is that the silence may be
telling us something about the kind of growth that survives.
- **Nick Bostrom (2003-04), "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?", Philosophical Quarterly 53 (211), 243–255 (2003).** Bostrom argues that at least one of three things is true: almost no civilisation reaches
the point of being able to run detailed simulations of its ancestors; almost none that
reach it choose to; or we are almost certainly living in such a simulation, because the
simulated people would vastly outnumber the real ones. The paper is not about the Fermi
question, but it is the source of the simulation explanation for the silence: if our
universe is a simulation, the sky may be empty because the simulators did not fill it,
or because rendering other civilisations was not worth the cost. The argument's first
two horns are themselves versions of the Great Filter and the inward turn.
- **Geoffrey A. Landis (1998), "The Fermi Paradox: An Approach Based on Percolation Theory", Journal of the British Interplanetary Society 51, 163–166 (1998).** Landis, a NASA physicist, models settlement of the galaxy the way physicists model a
liquid seeping through rock. Each settled star either sends out settlers to its
neighbours or does not, with some fixed probability; if that probability is below a
critical value, the settled region stops growing by itself and leaves large voids, and
if it is above, the galaxy fills. The point is that Hart's and Tipler's argument assumes
every colony keeps colonising, forever. Relax that one assumption and a galaxy can hold
many civilisations, some of them very old, with unsettled regions the size of our
neighbourhood, without any of them choosing to hide.
- **Glen David Brin (1983), "The 'Great Silence': the Controversy Concerning Extraterrestrial Intelligent Life", Quarterly Journal of the Royal Astronomical Society 24, 283–309 (1983).** The first full survey of the question, written by an astrophysicist who went on to be a
novelist. Brin coins 'the Great Silence' for the fact that needs explaining, lays out
the two camps (those who say the galaxy should be full and conclude it is empty, and
those who say the search has barely started), and then catalogues the explanations
offered to that point: colonisation that stalls, deadly self-replicating probes, worlds
covered by water whose inhabitants never build radios, civilisations that keep quiet out
of fear, zoos, and self-destruction. He does not pick one. His own contribution is the
observation that a galaxy with even one dangerous species in it would teach every other
species to be quiet, which is the dark forest argument twenty-five years early.
- **Frank J. Tipler (1980), "Extraterrestrial Intelligent Beings do not Exist", Quarterly Journal of the Royal Astronomical Society 21, 267–281 (1980).** Tipler sharpens Hart's argument by removing the need for anyone to travel. A civilisation
with our level of rocketry plus a machine able to copy itself from raw materials (the
self-reproducing probe that John von Neumann described) could send one probe, which
builds more at each star, and have a probe at every star in the galaxy within about 300
million years for an initial outlay smaller than running a radio beacon for a few centuries. Since no such probes are in the solar
system, he concludes that no other technological civilisation has ever arisen in the
galaxy, and that the radio search is founded on a mistake. The paper is where the
self-copying probe entered the debate, both as the reason the galaxy should be full and,
in later hands, as the thing that might be keeping it empty.
- **Michael H. Hart (1975), "An Explanation for the Absence of Extraterrestrials on Earth", Quarterly Journal of the Royal Astronomical Society 16, 128–135 (1975).** The paper that turned Fermi's lunchtime question into an argument. Hart starts from one
observation, that there are no intelligent beings from elsewhere on Earth now, which he
calls Fact A, and argues that a civilisation able to travel between stars at even a tenth
of the speed of light would cross most of the galaxy in about 650,000 years (about twice
that if there are pauses between voyages), so any civilisation that began exploring more
than about 2 million years ago would already have reached us. That is a blink against the
galaxy's age. He sorts every way out into four kinds (they cannot come, they
chose not to, they have not had time, they came and we missed them) and rejects each, and
concludes that we are the first civilisation in the galaxy and that searching for radio
signals is probably a waste of time and money.
- **John A. Ball (1973-07), "The Zoo Hypothesis", Icarus 19, 347–349 (1973).** A three-page note from a radio astronomer at Harvard's observatory proposing that the
galaxy may hold civilisations far older than ours that have deliberately set our region
aside, as a wilderness or a zoo, and avoid any contact that would disturb it. Ball's
point is that this is the only explanation he can find that fits both a galaxy old enough
to have been settled many times over and the fact that nobody is here; he admits it is
unfalsifiable and unpleasant, and says so. The paper named the idea that Star Trek had
already put on television as the Prime Directive, and it remains the reference every
later 'they are watching' explanation cites.
- **Frank Drake, and the Green Bank meeting: Dana Atchley, Melvin Calvin, Su-Shu Huang, John Lilly, Philip Morrison, Bernard Oliver, Carl Sagan, Otto Struve, J. Peter Pearman (1961-11), "The Drake Equation, as written for the Green Bank conference on extraterrestrial intelligent life", Order of Dolphin meeting, National Radio Astronomy Observatory, Green Bank, West Virginia, 1961-11-01 to 1961-11-03; first published account in J. P. T. Pearman, 'Extraterrestrial Intelligent Life and Interstellar Communication: An Informal Discussion', in Interstellar Communication, ed. A. G. W. Cameron (Benjamin, 1963), 287–293.** Drake wrote the equation on a blackboard as the agenda for a three-day meeting of about ten
people: the number of detectable civilisations in the galaxy equals the rate at which
suitable stars form, times the share with planets, times the habitable planets per such
star, times the share on which life starts, times the share where it becomes intelligent,
times the share that become detectable, times how long they stay so. The meeting's own
values, as later recounted, were about 1 to 10 stars a year, 0.2 to 0.5, 1 to 5, 1, 1, 0.1
to 0.2, and a lifetime of 1,000 to 100,000,000 years, which made the number of
civilisations roughly equal to the lifetime in years: somewhere between 1,000 and 100
million. The equation was never meant as a calculation; it was a way to organise an
argument, and it has done that job for sixty-five years.

## Factor 7 of 7: How long a civilisation stays detectable, in years (L)
Unit: years.

### What it is
Once a civilisation becomes detectable, how many years it stays that way before it dies out,
falls silent or changes into something we would not recognise. Multiplied by the rate at which
civilisations appear, this gives how many are detectable at any one moment, which is why Drake's
1961 meeting concluded that N is roughly equal to L. Our own technological civilisation has been
detectable for about a century. The published figures run from a few hundred years, read off
the lifetimes of historical empires, to billions of years for a civilisation that spreads
beyond its home planet; the gap between them is the single widest in the equation after the
chance of life starting.

### What would move this
A published argument with a number on how long technological civilisations last (from history, from risk studies of our own prospects, or from the physics of long-lived machines), or a detection whose age bounds it.

### The published estimates the record lists
1. **Frank Drake and the Green Bank meeting, 1961: 1,000 to 100,000,000.** Educated guess, from a thousand years for a civilisation that destroys itself to a hundred million for one that does not; the meeting's spread of N, 20 to 50 million, came almost entirely from this factor.
   Source: Green Bank meeting of November 1961, as recorded in Drake and Sobel, Is Anyone Out There? (Delacorte, 1992), pages 55 to 62 (drake-1961)

2. **J. Richard Gott, 1993-05: 5,100 to 7,800,000.** Reasoned that we are unlikely to be observing our species at an unusual moment of its history: given about 200,000 years so far, the human species should last between 5,100 and 7.8 million more years with 95% confidence. A statement about our species, not about its detectable phase, recorded because it is the best-known attempt to put a number on how long we have.
   Source: Gott, 'Implications of the Copernican principle for our future prospects', Nature 363, 315–319 (1993)
   Link: https://www.nature.com/articles/363315a0

3. **Michael Shermer, 2002-08: 304 to 420.** Averaged the recorded lifetimes of sixty historical civilisations on Earth, 420 years, and of the twenty-eight since the fall of Rome, 304 years (the column gives 420.6 and 304.5), and took that as the likely lifetime of a technological civilisation.
   Source: Shermer, 'Why ET hasn't called', Scientific American 287(2), 21 (2002)

4. **David Grinspoon, 2004: 1,000,000,000.** Argued that a civilisation which survives its first dangerous centuries may become effectively immortal, so that the factor should be replaced by the share of civilisations that become immortal times the age of the galaxy; a billion years is the figure the argument reaches.
   Source: Grinspoon, Lonely Planets: The Natural Philosophy of Alien Life (HarperCollins, 2004)

5. **Anders Sandberg, Eric Drexler and Toby Ord, 2018-06: 100 to 10,000,000,000.** A span evenly spread across its powers of ten, from the published estimates (which they read as 50 years to ten billion), cut off at the age of the galaxy because the equation assumes civilisations appear at a steady rate.
   Source: Sandberg, Drexler and Ord, 'Dissolving the Fermi Paradox', arXiv:1806.02404 (2018), Table 1 (sandberg-2018)
   Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.02404

6. **Tom Westby and Christopher Conselice, 2020-06: 100.** Assumed: the average lifetime of a communicating civilisation is taken to be 100 years, 'based on our own at present', and their count of 36 scales with it.
   Source: Westby and Conselice, 'The Astrobiological Copernican Weak and Strong Limits for Intelligent Life', Astrophysical Journal 896, 58 (2020) (westby-2020)
   Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.03968

The 2018 paper's span of 100 to ten billion years, kept as it is. The floor is the shortest

# From the method document: what the computation does

## What the computation does

The Drake Equation multiplies seven quantities: how many stars are born each year, the share with planets, the planets per star that could support life, the share of those where life starts, the share of those where it becomes intelligent, the share of intelligent species that are detectable, and how long they stay detectable. Each factor's record in `data/factors/` carries a range and a shape. One run draws one value from every range and multiplies the seven. The equation is played out two hundred thousand times, and the headline numbers are read off the spread of results:

1. **The middle estimate** of detectable civilisations in the galaxy: the median of the runs. Not the mean, which the rare very optimistic runs pull up to millions (the 2018 paper's mean was 27 million against a median of 0.32).
2. **The chance we are alone in the galaxy**: the share of runs in which the result came out below one. (The exact chance that a galaxy with an expected N civilisations has none is e to the minus N; the 2018 paper's footnote 3 shows the difference from the share below one is about one percentage point, and we follow them in using the share.)
3. **The chance we are alone in the observable universe**: the share of runs in which the result came out below one per the number of Milky-Way-sized galaxies' worth of stars we can see.
4. **The width of the argument**: the result when every factor takes its most optimistic published estimate at once, and when every factor takes its most pessimistic. Computed from the estimates in the records, as the kickoff defines it; the same figure from the ranges' ends is reported beside it.

Three shapes are allowed by the record layout: `log-uniform` (every power of ten between the ends is equally likely, right for a quantity whose uncertainty is "which power of ten"), `uniform` (every value equally likely), and `log-normal-rate`, the 2018 paper's model for the chance life starts (item 7 below), which has no ends: the number of times life starts on a habitable planet over its life is drawn from a bell curve in its powers of ten, and the chance is one minus e to the minus that number. Six ranges are log-uniform; the chance life starts uses the third shape since John's ruling of 2026-10-16.

Where a low and a high are needed for a range with no ends (the band on the calculator, the width from the ranges' ends), the pessimistic end is taken as the chance one spread below the centre (about one in ten to the fifty for the paper's shape) and the optimistic end as 1; where a starting value is needed (the calculator's slider), the chance at the centre, 0.63. These are display conveniences, written in `scripts/compute.py` as `range_ends` and `range_middle`.
