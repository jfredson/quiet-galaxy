# The review brief

Sent unchanged at the top of every packet, so the answers are comparable. Drafted 2026-10-17 with the protocol (`docs/reviews/protocol.md`); the first review ran under it the same day.

---

You are reviewing part of The Quiet Galaxy, a public site that tracks the Drake Equation: the seven quantities that, multiplied together, give the number of civilisations in our galaxy that could be detected right now. The site does not pick one value for each quantity. Each of the seven carries a range with a shape, and the equation is played out two hundred thousand times with a random draw from every range, after the 2018 paper by Sandberg, Drexler and Ord, "Dissolving the Fermi Paradox". The headline numbers (the middle estimate, the chance we are alone in the galaxy, the chance we are alone in the observable universe) are read off that spread.

Every range was proposed by one AI model (Claude) on 2026-10-16 from the published estimates and accepted by the author as a first pass. Nothing is promoted past "first pass" until a model from another lab has challenged the ranges. Your job is critique, not agreement. Plain language throughout; a reader outside the field should follow every sentence.

Below are the factor records for this packet, as the site stores them: each quantity's description, the published estimates it lists (who, when, the value or the span, the source, and how it was reached), the range the site uses now with its shape and the reason for it, the notes, the revisions, the claims still to be checked against a source, and the publications the site's weekly reading has logged against the factor. After the records comes the part of the method that says what the three shapes mean and how a draw is made.

For every factor in the packet, answer four questions.

1. **The shape.** Is the shape right for this quantity? The site allows three: log-uniform (every power of ten between the ends is equally likely; right when the uncertainty is "which power of ten"), uniform (every value between the ends equally likely), and log-normal-rate (used only for the chance life starts: the number of times life starts on a habitable planet over its life is drawn from a bell curve in its powers of ten, with a centre and a spread, and the chance is one minus e to the minus that number). If you would change the shape, say to which and why.
2. **The ends.** Are the low and the high defensible against the estimates the record lists and against the published measurements you know of? Say where each end should be and why. Raise an end only if you would move it by at least a factor of two; smaller moves are not worth arguing.
3. **What is missing.** Name any published estimate or measurement that bears on the range and is not in the record's estimate list: who, when, the figure, and where it was published. Say whether it would move the range, and which way.
4. **What would move it.** Is the record's "what would move this" line the right test? If a different kind of result would move the range sooner, say what.

Two factors carry a further question, to be answered when the packet holds them.

- **The chance life starts (fl).** The shape is settled: the author ruled on 2026-10-16 that the 2018 paper's log-normal rate is used, and that ruling is not reopened here. What is open is the centre (the paper's is one start per habitable planet over its life) and the spread (the paper's is fifty powers of ten). Give the centre and the spread you would use and the reason. Raise the centre only if you would move it by at least two powers of ten, and the spread only if you would move it by at least ten powers of ten.
- **How many stars are born each year (R\*).** The record's top is 10 stars a year. A 2023 measurement from the galaxy's glow of radioactive aluminium (Siegert and others) gives 4 to 8 solar masses of new stars a year, about 8 to 16 stars a year at half a solar mass per star, above the top; a 2025 census of massive stars near the Sun (Quintana and others) gives 0.67 solar masses a year, about 1.3 stars a year, inside it. Say whether the top should rise past 10, to what, and why; and whether the floor of 1 holds.

**Not asked:** the headline numbers, the site, the method's rule for combining the seven (independent draws, the number of runs, the count of galaxies for the observable universe), and the catalogue of explanations for the silence. If you think one of those is wrong, say so in one line at the end and no more; they get their own review.

Give your answer in this form.

- First, a table with one row per item you raise: item number, factor, what it concerns (shape, low end, high end, centre, spread, missing estimate, test), your proposal in a few words, your confidence in it (low, medium, high).
- Then the items in order, each a short paragraph: what the record says now, what you would change and to what, the reason, and what evidence would settle it.
- Then one line per factor in the packet that you would leave as it is, saying so and why.

You may look things up. If you do, say where in the item, so facts from lookup can be told from your judgment. Give a source for any figure you add: who, when, where published. Do not soften findings to be polite; a factor with nothing to challenge is a possible finding, but an unlikely one.
